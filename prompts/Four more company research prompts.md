# Four investigation prompts

Short briefs for leading-edge chip fabrication and lithography, AI processors, long-term energy and quantum computing. Each starts from the technology, asks which companies have a coherent path forward, and leaves the form of the answer to the researcher.

## 1. Leading-edge chip fabrication and lithography

Investigate the companies that manufacture the most advanced chips, and those that supply the lithography, other equipment and materials this depends on. Look worldwide, at listed and private companies, and take challengers and early-stage efforts as seriously as the leaders.

Start from the technology. Establish where each company really stands on the steps ahead: the next process generations, high-numerical-aperture lithography and what follows it, stacked transistors, rear-side power, and the yield learning each step demands. Weigh what AI changes: it drives the demand, it multiplies the number of custom chip designs, and it is entering the fab itself through faster computational lithography, automated measurement and process tuning. Consider export controls and the efforts in China, Japan and elsewhere to build independent capability. Pay attention to how each claim gets proven: what simulation can settle, what needs wafers through a fab, and how long that takes.

Then tell me which companies have a coherent path forward. Challengers often fail, so weigh the risk against the size of the opportunity and the potential return instead of ruling them out. Rank the companies by quality, judged for their stage. Keep that apart from whether today's price is a sensible entry point, and give me both. Separately, name the two companies you would consider shorting, and why. Rely on evidence you can cite, and say where you are unsure or where you disagree with the common view.

Companies to include, among any others you find: TSMC, Samsung Electronics, Intel, Rapidus, SMIC; ASML, Nikon, Canon, Carl Zeiss, Trumpf, SMEE, xLight, Substrate; Applied Materials, Lam Research, Tokyo Electron, ASM International, Screen Holdings, Kokusai Electric, Naura, AMEC; JSR, Tokyo Ohka Kogyo, Shin-Etsu Chemical, Hoya, Tekscend Photomask.

## 2. AI processors

Investigate the companies that make the chips running AI in data centres: processors sold on the open market, the custom chips that large buyers design for themselves, the design partners that help build them, and start-ups with new architectures. Look worldwide, at listed and private companies, and take early-stage ventures as seriously as established ones.

Start from the technology. Establish what each design actually delivers per watt and per dollar on real workloads, and what holds it back: memory, packaging, the links between chips, software. Weigh what the pace of AI itself changes: models are replaced every few months, software efficiency is improving faster than hardware, AI now helps design and verify chips, and the work is shifting from training models to running them. Ask whether this favours general-purpose processors, buyers' own chips or specialised newcomers. Pay attention to how each claim gets proven: independent measurements against the company's own figures, and silicon in customers' hands against announcements.

Then tell me which companies have a coherent path forward. Newcomers often fail, so weigh the risk against the size of the opportunity and the potential return instead of ruling them out. Rank the companies by quality, judged for their stage. Keep that apart from whether today's price is a sensible entry point, and give me both. Separately, name the two companies you would consider shorting, and why. Rely on evidence you can cite, and say where you are unsure or where you disagree with the common view.

Companies to include, among any others you find: NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, Qualcomm; Broadcom, Marvell, MediaTek, Alchip, Global Unichip, Socionext; Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta as chip designers; Huawei, Cambricon, Hygon, Alibaba T-Head; Cerebras, Groq, SambaNova, Tenstorrent, Etched, d-Matrix, Taalas, Lightmatter.

## 3. Long-term energy

Investigate the companies building long-term energy supply: fusion, advanced and small nuclear reactors, the nuclear fuel chain, enhanced geothermal, long-duration storage, and the grid equipment that connects them. Look worldwide, at listed and private companies, and take early-stage ventures as seriously as established ones.

Start from the technology. For each approach, establish what has actually been demonstrated, what still has to be proven, and what each company's timetable requires. Weigh what AI changes: its demand for electricity is funding these companies through long-term power contracts, and AI is now used on the machines themselves, in reactor and stellarator design, plasma control and faster simulation. Pay attention to how each claim gets proven: what simulation can settle, what needs a working plant or a regulator's licence, and how long that takes.

Then tell me which companies have a coherent path forward: technically, financially, and with regulators and customers. Many early ventures fail, so weigh the risk against the size of the opportunity and the potential return instead of ruling them out. Rank the companies by quality, judged for their stage. Keep that apart from whether today's price is a sensible entry point, and give me both. Separately, name the two companies you would consider shorting, and why. Rely on evidence you can cite, and say where you are unsure or where you disagree with the common view.

Companies to include, among any others you find: Commonwealth Fusion Systems, Helion, TAE Technologies, General Fusion, Tokamak Energy, Type One Energy, Thea Energy, Proxima Fusion, Energy Singularity; TerraPower, X-energy, Kairos Power, Oklo, NuScale Power, newcleo, Rolls-Royce, GE Vernova, Westinghouse, BWX Technologies, Doosan Enerbility, China National Nuclear Corporation; Cameco, Centrus Energy, Kazatomprom; Fervo Energy, Ormat Technologies, Quaise Energy, Eavor; Form Energy, Fluence, CATL; Siemens Energy, Hitachi, Prysmian.

## 4. Quantum computing

Investigate the companies building quantum computers, and the hardware, control systems and software they depend on. Look worldwide, at listed and private companies, and take early-stage ventures as seriously as established ones.

Start from the technology. For each approach (superconducting, trapped-ion, neutral-atom, photonic, spin and others), establish what has actually been demonstrated in error-corrected operation, what still has to be proven, and what each company's roadmap requires. Weigh what AI changes, in both directions: AI decoders, automated calibration and AI-designed circuits are reducing what a useful machine needs, and classical AI is also competing for some of the problems quantum computers were meant to solve. Pay attention to how each claim gets proven: results that outside groups have checked against the company's own announcements.

Then tell me which companies have a coherent path forward, technically and financially. Many will fail, so weigh the risk against the size of the opportunity and the potential return instead of ruling them out. Rank the companies by quality, judged for their stage. Keep that apart from whether today's price is a sensible entry point, and give me both. Separately, name the two companies you would consider shorting, and why. Rely on evidence you can cite, and say where you are unsure or where you disagree with the common view.

Companies to include, among any others you find: IBM, Google Quantum AI, Microsoft, Amazon, Quantinuum, IonQ, Rigetti Computing, D-Wave Quantum, Infleqtion, Xanadu, Quantum Computing Inc., QuEra, Pasqal, Atom Computing, PsiQuantum, IQM, Alice & Bob, Oxford Quantum Circuits, Quantum Motion, Diraq, Origin Quantum, Fujitsu, Quantum Machines, Q-CTRL, Riverlane, Bluefors.
