Welcome and Monday Plenary Session

Strategic directions for electronics packaging

Subramanian S. Iyer

                                                    Show abstract

                                                        Recent advances in electronics packaging have come to the rescue as CMOS scaling has stalled making possible the incredible advances in Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning that promise to transform our lives. This journey, however, has only just begun and much more is yet to come. The key features that will drive this transformation can be described with the simple strategy of “scale-down and scale-out” that has characterized monolithic CMOS scaling for several decades, the drive to chiplets with higher yields, and the ability to assemble a diversity of technologies on the same substrate allowing us to blur the lines between monolithic chip and a large heterogeneous assembly of chips. While, we have made progress towards this goal, the technologies we have developed have ridden on legacy packaging technologies making such systems incredibly complex and expensive to build. In this talk we will describe our approach to simplify packaging at all levels: from design, architecture, process and manufacturing that have the potential to take packaging to the next level including the ability to scale packaging systematically. There are many challenges in this approach. In this talk we will outline these challenges especially in lithography and die placement and show that the adoption of silicon like technology, new cooling and power delivery, large area lithography approaches, as well as design enablement will propel packaging into the next dimension.

Keynotes

Evolution of in-fab e-beam inspection technology and its role in the future (from 2D to 4D)

Younghoon Sohn
, 

Minchul Yoon
, 

Minho Rim
, 

et al.

                                                    Show abstract

                                                        As the semiconductor process becomes more delicate, defects to be detected in the fab face more challenges. Moreover, as the process becomes more complex, the types of defects that occur during the process, from physical dimension defects to chemical characteristic defects, are also diversifying. In order to detect such defects, technology has evolved into two directions: improving the resolution performance of technologies suitable for defect characteristics and developing detection performance according to the characteristics of defects using e-Beam technology with the best resolution in existence. In this paper, we will look at the evolution of the defect detection method of e-Beam technology, which is the second direction above, and look at the cases used in the actual fab. e-Beam technology started with fundamental CD measurement and is now expanding its area by examining various defects. In particular, the inspection area has evolved from the two-dimensional physical defect detection to chemical property defects, electrical short, and electrical cut defects. In addition, it is developing as three-dimensional structure inspecting technique using the property that the electron interacts with the substructure. In order to overcome the speed limitation, which is the biggest problem when it comes to mass production monitoring, H/W, S/W and methodological methods have also developed. In this paper, we will examine the evolution of these technologies from 2D to 4D, the latest high technology in each category, and some results of verifying the performance through cases applied to actual products.

3D metrology and inspection to enable the rise of stacked transistors, wafers, and chips

J. Bogdanowicz
, 

A.-L. Charley
, 

P. Leray
, 

et al.

                                                    Show abstract

                                                        A critical trend in today’s semiconductor technology is the usage of the 3rd, i.e. vertical, dimension. Although lateral x-y scaling is alive and kicking as demonstrated by the ongoing deployment of High Numerical-Aperture EUV lithography, leveraging the vertical dimension as an extra degree of freedom to squeeze more functionality into the same Si surface area is gaining immense traction. The introduction of 3DNAND flash memories a decade ago only announced the rise of stacked devices, wafers and chips. Today, examples of monolithic and sequential stacking are indeed countless, with applications in both the memory and logic segments and obviously in 3D integration which is its cornerstone. This paper presents our views on the implications of this technology trend on the field of semiconductor metrology and inspection (M&I). We conclude with a call for more collaboration within the M&I ecosystem to provide and test new techniques, which appears to us a critical condition to the further deployment of 3D devices.

Optical Metrology

Full-wafer 3D metrology solution based on imaging ellipsometry for process uniformity control

Hyukjoon Cho
, 

Yong-Ju Jeon
, 

Jhongkwon Kim
, 

et al.

                                                    Show abstract

                                                        As semiconductor manufacturing technology becomes more complex, process control becomes more important, and in particular, technologies for discovering and controlling structural defects become more important. However, considering the productivity of manufacturing, it is impossible to achieve full coverage measurement with existing measurement technologies. Here, we propose imaging ellipsometry-based full wafer coverage metrology (IFM) that acquires spectra of all cell blocks of DRAM devices using large-area imaging ellipsometry measurements with a 22 mm x 22 mm field of view (FOV) and 6.5 μm spatial resolution. A prediction model learned from tens to hundreds of reference data points and the acquired spectra helps generate a massive amount of measurement data exceeding 12 million points per wafer. The vast amount of data generated allows us to extend the spatial coverage of existing thickness measurements, which typically cover only 0.001%, to up to 47%. The proposed method can play a critical role in detecting suspected equipment failures, especially when repetitive and periodic patterns indicating problems appear in the fault map. This novel approach identified and mitigated equipment-induced distributions that negatively impacted product yield that were difficult to observe using conventional metrology. The effectiveness of this approach was verified through measurements performed over 100 steps on the DRAM device.

Critical in-line OCD metrology for CFET manufacturing

Hyukyun Kwon
, 

Joey Hung
, 

Adam Michal Urbanowicz
, 

et al.

                                                    Show abstract

                                                        As semiconductor technology advances, scaling transistors becomes increasingly challenging. The difficulty in defining and scaling devices is compounded by short channel effects, parasitic effects, and metal resistance, which limit gate-length scaling, device density, and metal pitch. Consequently, the complementary FET (CFET) architecture offers a solution by vertically stacking nFET and pFET transistors, thereby overcoming the limitations of n-to-p separation. This 'folding' approach reduces the cell active area footprint, making CFET a promising candidate for next-generation semiconductor nodes. However, CFET adoption introduces complexities in manufacturing and process control. Tight control is needed for the Si/SiGe superlattices with multiple layers and different Ge content, both for the CFET performance and the novel middle-dielectric- insulation (MDI) process. A higher aspect ratio is used in all front-end patterning applications. The multiple novels etch-back steps require tight vertical edge placement error (vEPE). Inner spacer uniformity control also becomes more challenging. In-line Optical Critical Dimension (OCD) is essential to control all the complex processes involved in complementary FET (CFET) manufacturing, enabling fast, non-destructive, and precise tracking of all critical parameters, enabling reduction of the wafer-to-wafer and within-the-wafer variations. The current paper presents OCD monitoring results for the development of the essential CFET process steps, starting from the superlattice formation down to the inner spacer (ISP) etch back. The required robust OCD models were created for all process steps based on the specially designed splits defining process windows, validated with the reference metrology, and applied to the pilot line process control.

Optimized EUV scatterometry measurements with tunable high harmonic generation and the Fisher information matrix

Clay Klein
, 

Nicholas Jenkins
, 

Yunzhe Shao
, 

et al.

                                                    Show abstract

                                                        We present the use of high harmonic generation (HHG) to achieve sub-nanometer sensitivity to out-of-plane geometric features on an industrially relevant damascene sample with extreme ultraviolet (EUV) scatterometry. We also demonstrate a methodology to take advantage of the tuneability of HHG by selecting experimental conditions that maximize the sensitivity of EUV scatterometry. The method uses the Fisher information matrix to encode prior knowledge about the sample into a robust mathematical framework that enables automated selection of the optimal experimental conditions such as wavelength and incidence angles. Importantly, HHG allows us to use wavelength tunability across the EUV spectrum through precisely controlled phase matching to achieve these conditions. We apply this to our damascene sample to show that EUV light at ~13nm will maximize the sensitivity of our scatterometry measurements and the angle selection will break correlations between parameters to enable a reliable solution to the inverse problem. Using this approach, we are able to take full advantage of the wavelength tunability of HHG based EUV sources to support accurate metrology for next generation semiconductor devices.

Phase retrieval of periodic patterns

Paolo Ansuinelli
, 

Hojun Lee
, 

Wookrae Kim
, 

et al.

                                                    Show abstract

                                                        Coherent Diffraction Imaging (CDI) is a promising method for the metrology of lithographic structures due its low cost, ease of implementation, and scalability. CDI can be realized in different ways, but the core process involves illuminating the sample with coherent light, recording the diffracted intensity distribution, and computationally recovering the phase to reconstruct the complex amplitude of the sample. One of the primary challenges in semiconductor metrology is high resolution inspection for lithographic patterns. Although the patterns can be relatively complex, periodic structures are found in many layers of modern devices. Given the traction gained by hybrid bonding for wafer–to–wafer (W2W) or chip–to–wafer (C2W) interconnects, their metrology is becoming important. It is interesting to study and research phase retrieval methods that can potentially be applied to reconstruct the topography of the Cu pads structures that are used for the electrical connections in such scenario. This paper presents a study of phase retrieval algorithms applied to reconstruct the profile of the dishing/protrusions present on each Cu pillar of the array. In particular, we explore a sample model featuring a Cu pad array where the single pillars have a bowl-shaped topography and we investigate, by means of simulations, the accuracy that we can achieve with this approach in the measurement of the depth and shape of the concavity of the surface of the pillars.

Ellipsometry in the EUV regime

Aaron Chew
, 

Bryan M. Barnes
, 

Eric L. Shirley
, 

et al.

                                                    Show abstract

                                                        As semiconducting devices grow smaller in scale, measuring their critical dimensions becomes more challenging. Knowledge of these dimensions is crucial, as several of these parameters control the quality of the transistors being manufactured. A popular non-destructive measurement technique is ellipsometry, where polarized light is diffracted off of the grating and the diffraction intensities are fit against a simulated model. Visible light, however, is unable to decorrelate parameters for the smallest and most complex devices, requiring the development of ellipsometers that operate at shorter wavelengths. We conduct a sensitivity analysis of a new ellipsometer designed and being built by NIST. This ellipsometer uses extreme ultraviolet (EUV) radiation from NIST’s SURF-III synchrotron. Its distinguishing feature is the use of two rotating four-mirror devices to control polarization. The rotating compensators allow the ellipsometer to probe the entire Mueller matrix in the EUV. With a combination of Monte Carlo simulations and least squares fitting, we demonstrate the superior sensitivity of an ellipsometer employing both EUV radiation and polarization control. We simulate ellipsometry experiments with several industrially relevant geometries, and observe the parametric decorrelation of important parameters with shorter wavelength. We also observe the benefits of employing phase control for certain geometries, like stacked thin films. Numerical and analytical justification is offered for this improved sensitivity.

Electron Beam Metrology

On-product, on-device overlay breakdown by layer and contributor using a high-voltage scanning electron microscope

Stefan Eyring
, 

Deepak Selvanathan
, 

Amrita Basu
, 

et al.

                                                    Show abstract

                                                        Meeting stricter on-product overlay requirements necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the factors that contribute to overlay errors. A detailed examination of the overlay error contributors at the wafer-level, field-level, and highly localized level, which can vary over the micrometer length scale, forms the basis for such improvement. In this paper, we investigate on-target and on-device overlay across different length scales and time domains using a high-voltage scanning electron microscope (HV-SEM) that employs KLA’s model-based e-beam physics algorithm (ePhysX™) for our analyses. We analyze the individual overlay error contributors by tracking multiple wafers through the process, applying a novel method to measure overlay post-litho inspection (ADI) and after-etch inspection (AEI) at multiple layer steps. Our study encompasses the effects from multiple scanners, lots, and wafers. Additionally, we account for mask errors by measuring pattern placement on the masks used for wafer exposure with a mask registration tool. Using this data, we perform a breakdown of the overlay errors into individual contributors, such as target overlay, device overlay, non-zero-offset (NZO) between target overlay and device overlay, and pattern placement errors on the masks. For each contributor, we examine sub-components in both the time domain (e.g., static, wafer-to-wafer and lot-to-lot) and the spatial domain (e.g., wafer-level and field-level) using novel analysis methods to identify the root causes of different effects. This approach enables us to pinpoint potential overlay improvements that remain uncorrected. For example, contributions, on field-level or micro-scale level, which are static over time, can be corrected by implementing a reticle writing correction on an updated mask. For contributions that are dynamically changing over time, this method can help to identify the root causes.

Beyond 3 sigma: roughness metrology evolution at the last 20 years (in memory of Ben Bunday)

Roman Kris
, 

Shahar Arad
, 

Shimon Levi
, 

et al.

                                                    Show abstract

                                                        The importance of Critical Dimension (CD) roughness metrics, including line edge and contact edge roughness (LER, CER) and their associated feature dimension metrics line width and contact diameter roughness (LWR, CWR), has been acknowledged in the semiconductor industry automatic process control (APC) at early stage of CD SEM (Scanning Electron Microscope) Metrology. The evolution of roughness metrology follows three strategical directions: the enhancing of roughness metrics for comprehensive automatic process control in advanced technological nodes, development of precise algorithms to remove apparatus bias for accurate roughness estimation and extensive search of roughness metrics related applications for yield increase through their use in Process Window definition. The roughness metrics development from the standard deviation based to the advanced measurements challenging by technology development for 3D NAND and under 20 nm nodes is described.
