Abstract

Wafer bonding is a step in processing of state-of-the-art integration architectures in CMOS devices. Sufficiently high bonding strength and low distortion with high alignment accuracy are essential to realize these device structures. A challenge in realizing advanced architectures is reducing the thermal history associated with the bonding process. Although much research has been conducted on wafer bonding methods compatible with the latest semiconductor manufacturing processes, discussions on the interface mechanisms during low temperature annealing have been insufficient. In this study, plasma-activated bonding was carried out using SiCN, which is a major bonding dielectric material. The bonding strength and water remaining at the interface were subsequently evaluated. We found that a SiCN film achieved greater bonding strength after post bond annealing at a low temperature of 250 °C and completely consumed the interfacial water. Analyses of the surface and interface revealed the carbon bonding leads to great bonding interface by low-temperature annealing.

1. Introduction

Advanced 3D integration structures such as complementary field-effect transistors (CFETs) and backside power delivery networks (BSPDNs) have been progressively investigated to achieve greater integrity and lower power consumption per unit area in advanced processors. − Direct wafer bonding is essential for these architectures, and minimizing distortion at the bonding step is important to ensure overlay accuracy in lithography during backside fabrication. Thus, an atomic-level understanding of direct bonding technology is essential for the advanced-node complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS) process era. In addition, hybrid bonding plays an important role in 3D chiplet applications that require high-density connections between wafers or chips, such as memory stacking and sensor integration. , In such applications, highly reliable bonding with extreme grid alignment and sufficient bonding strength is essential to maintain high yields, where high thermal, electrical, and mechanical reliability are required for the bonding dielectric film. In addition, the thermal history resulting from high temperatures during bonding processes is becoming a serious issue in maintaining device performance. , Therefore, achieving high bonding strength and reliable interfaces at low temperatures is the key to the further development of 3D structures fabricated using bonding processes.

SiO2 deposited by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) has been mainly used in the direct bonding process for CMOS processes. However, the strength of the resultant bond is insufficient when postbonding annealing (PBA) is conducted at a temperature of less than 250 °C. In addition, CVD-SiO2 has excess water at the interface, resulting in the formation of microvoids, which adversely affect the strength of bonds formed in low-temperature processes. Overcoming this problem requires the development of new materials that form a water-free interface that can maintain stable bonding strength after low-temperature annealing.

Recently, CVD-SiCN has attracted attention as an alternative material for achieving low-temperature bonds with the desired properties. CVD-SiCN has been shown to maintain high bonding strength after low-temperature annealing and to exhibit superior void suppression capability compared with conventional CVD-SiO2. − SiCN is also known to function as a capping film against Cu in the back-end of line processes. These properties make CVD-SiCN a promising option for improving the reliability of devices fabricated using low-temperature bonding processes. However, there are many unexplored aspects of the mechanism involving SiCN, especially the specific influence of plasma activation conditions on the bonding strength. Previous studies have partially clarified the effects of dangling bonds and surface activation at the SiCN bonding interface. − However, the full extent of the factors contributing to the strength enhancement at low temperatures has not been elucidated, nor has the role of interfacial water.

In the present study, the mechanism resulting in a high bonding strength and a water-free interface in the low-temperature bonding of SiCN is clarified. From these findings, process factors that result in a more reliable bonding interface are proposed. Specifically, the conditions that promote the formation of an ideal interface are identified through a detailed evaluation of the changes in bonding strength under various plasma activation conditions. The aim is to provide, as a contribution to the further development of advanced integration technology, new knowledge for improving the reliability of low-temperature bonding processes using plasma activation.

2. Experimental Section

SiCN films were deposited onto 300 mm Si wafers via CVD, achieving a target thickness of 100 nm. Each wafer was subjected to chemical mechanical polishing (CMP) to obtain a surface roughness less than Rq 0.15 nm and the 80 nm film thickness, where the deposition parameters were adjusted to produce specific film properties. After CMP, N2 or O2 plasma activation and a deionized water rinse (hydration) were conducted. Finally, wafer bonding was performed. PBA was performed under nitrogen ambient at 200 °C, 250 °C, or 300 °C for 2 h.

Composition analysis was carried out with Rutherford backscattering spectrometry with elastic recoil detection analysis (RBS–ERDA, HRBS500, Kobelco). The refractive indexes of SiCN films were measured by ellipsometry (RC2X-XNIR, J.A. Woollam). Gas emissions from the films were analyzed using thermal desorption spectroscopy (TDS, TDS1200II, ESCO). ,

The double cantilever beam (DCB) test was conducted using a custom-designed apparatus to assess bonding strength. In this setup, an actuator-driven blade is introduced into the bonding interface with an adjustable insertion distance and speed to precisely initiate delamination. This semiautomated approach enables consistent and reproducible measurements of bonding strength. The bonding energy Gc was determined using eq

Gc=3Etb2tw3/16L4(J/m2) 1

where t b represents the blade thickness, E is the Young’s modulus, t w is the wafer thickness, and L is the delamination length.

The behavior of water stress corrosion manifests as an increase in the delamination distance over time after the blade is inserted. Internal water stress corrosion particularly occurs during measurements conducted in an anhydrous atmosphere (N2) inside a glovebox. This phenomenon can be used to evaluate remaining water at the bonding interface. In the present study, 0 s was defined as the moment the blade stopped, and the bonding strength was measured at 15, 30, 60, 120, and 180 s. The evaluation of interfacial remaining water was based on the reduction in bonding strength observed over these time intervals.

Chemical bonds at the surface of SiCN were investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Quantera-SXM, ULVAC-PHI). The number of dangling bonds inside SiCN films was evaluated by electron spin resonance (ESR, EMXplus, Bruker) spectroscopy. , Atomic-level vacancy analysis of bare SiCN films and their bonding interfaces was performed by positron annihilation spectroscopy (PAS). , Cross-sectional images were acquired by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM F200, JEOL), and additional interface composition and binding analysis were carried out by electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS, ARM200F, JEOL). In addition, after debonding, XPS analysis was performed to characterize interfacial bonds.

Figure a shows the refractive index measurement results for the SiCN films, as obtained by ellipsometry. The results show that the refractive index of SiCN-B is lower than that of SiCN-A, suggesting the presence of void defects or more impurities such as water in SiCN-B than in SiCN-A.

TDS measurements were also carried out to obtain information about impurities. Figure b shows the results of water desorption measurements for SiCN films. The spectrum of SiCN-B exhibits a peak at ∼250 °C, and the spectra of both films show relatively larger peaks at ∼350 °C compared with the peak in the spectrum of thermally grown SiO2 (Th–SiO2) in the previous report. The water desorption event near 250 °C corresponds to the direct desorption of moisture such as water vapor contained within the film, whereas the peak corresponding to 350 °C is associated with dehydration reactions occurring within the film. ,

The underlying reason for the disparity in detection between O and H2O in ERDA and TDS, respectively, can be attributed to the differing levels of adsorption or trapping of these elements within the SiCN film. The presence of H2O and OH, which exhibit weaker adsorption or trapping, renders them challenging to detect by ERDA. In contrast, elements that are strongly bound to each other, such as the film itself, are more readily detected. Consequently, these findings do not contradict each other, but rather provide complementary insights into the weakly trapped H2O in the SiCN film.

3.2. Characterization of Bonding Strength and Interfacial Remaining Water by DCB

The two of prepared SiCN-deposited wafers were bonded to each other via plasma activation. Figure a shows an image gained by scanning acoustic microscopy (SAM) of the SiCN-B wafer in its as-bonded condition; SiCN-A exhibited the same interface quality. The thermal stability of SiCN is consistent with that noted in previous reports. ,, Figure b shows the results of the bonding strength measurements on the laminated wafers treated at each of the investigated PBA temperatures. This result shows that the bonding strength of SiCN-B is higher than that of SiCN-A when PBA is conducted in the low-temperature region below 250 °C. The results also show that the bonding strength tends to be higher when N2 plasma is used than when O2 plasma is used. In particular, the bonding strength of SiCN-B wafers bonded using N2 plasma and subjected to PBA at 200 °C is substantially greater than that of those treated under the same PBA conditions.

This higher bonding strength might be due to the properties of the SiCN-B film and the surface state formed by N2 plasma, which promotes bond formation at the interface. That is, the specific processing conditions can enable interfacial covalent bonds such as Si–O–Si to form with less energy. It should be important for achieving a high bonding strength at low temperatures. These conditions will be analyzed in greater detail in later sections. The slight decrease in bonding strength at PBA 300 °C on SiCN-B with N2 plasma and reverse with SiCN-A are thought to be due to the interface expansion caused by the growth of the interface oxide layer. It opposes the interfacial gap closure, because excessive heating energy can cause too much oxide growth, which adversely affects its bonding strength reported in the prior study. It is also conceivable that rise of O-related bonds may disturb interfacial covalent bonds. More detailed measurements are needed to determine these mechanisms. The important thing here is that this decline is not so problematic, because it is negligible and has been demonstrated to be no longer worse at elevated temperatures.

We subsequently investigated the evolution of bonding strength over time to assess the remaining water at the interface. All of the measurements were conducted under anhydrous conditions; therefore, no water stress corrosion was caused by the air as the previous report. The detailed mechanism by which remaining water was detected (i.e., internal water stress corrosion) at the interface has been explained in our previous report. It is important to note of the assumption that less than 5% falls are within the 3σ error range, and the interfacial water consumption is completed from the point of compatibility with the high bonding strength. Figure shows the results of the time-dependent bonding strength measurements for SiCN-A and SiCN-B bonded via N2 plasma activation and subjected to different PBA temperatures. Figure a shows that the attenuation of SiCN-A was 38.5% when PBA was conducted at 200 °C and 21.8% when PBA was conducted at 250 °C. However, the attenuation of SiCN-A was 2.75% when PBA was conducted 300 °C. Figure b shows that SiCN-B also exhibited a large attenuation of 32.2% after PBA at 200 °C. However, after PBA at 250 °C, the attenuation was almost negligible (1.30%). Figure c summarizes the bonding strength degradation rate after 180 s compared with that at 0 s for wafers bonded under various process conditions. When O2 plasma was used, the PBA temperature at which the bonding strength attenuation disappears increased for both films. Nevertheless, the bonding strength attenuation for SiCN-B was found to be eliminated at lower temperatures than that for SiCN-A even in this case. The bonding strength attenuation for SiCN-B was also found to be eliminated at lower temperatures than that for Th–SiO2. It appears that PBA at 250 °C in SiCN-A with O2 plasma may be more degraded than PBA200 °C, but this is not the case. This is because the bonding strength is not high at PBA200 °C, and the reduction range of bonding strength is so small.

The aforementioned results indicate that SiCN-B does not exhibit a decrease in bonding strength at lower temperatures. SiCN-B can consume the remaining water at the interface, consistent with the high bonding strength observed for SiCN-B at low temperatures in Figure b. This result is counterintuitive because SiCN-B contains more water than Th–SiO2. Conversely, the results suggest that there may be a dynamism that consumes a large amount of water. The higher interfacial water consumption capacity of SiCN-B compared with that of Th–SiO2 suggests that SiCN-B not only forms siloxane bonds via Si dangling bonds and annealing-induced water diffusion but also consumes water via SiCN-specific C dangling bonds. The mechanism of water consumption by SiCN is considered to play a prominent role. , SiCN-A is considered to be inferior to SiCN-B with respect to its ability to consume water. The greater consumption of interfacial water by the N2-plasma-activated samples compared with the O2-plasma-activated samples indicates that the surface irradiated with N2 plasma differs from that treated with O2 plasma. For example, N-related bonds might be more likely to form interfacial bonds at lower temperatures or N2 plasma might create ideal surface conditions for interfacial water consumption. These consumption mechanisms will be discussed in subsequent sections; we focus here on SiCN-B. Therefore, more detailed investigations of SiCN-A and other films are needed to confirm the reproducibility of these interfacial conditions.

3.3. Analysis of Prebonding Surface

To investigate the properties regarding the water consumption mechanism, we used various measurements to characterize the film surfaces. Figure shows the XPS spectra of the SiCN-B surface before and after plasma irradiation. Before plasma irradiation, Si–N and SiCO bonds in the SiCN film itself are dominant. However, SiO bonds are dominant after plasma irradiation, irrespective of the plasma irradiation conditions. A comparison of the plasma treatments confirms the presence of a large proportion of Si–N bonding as a result of the introduction of N by the N2 plasma, as well as a large proportion of SiCO bonds.

The aforementioned results indicate that plasma forms Si–OH active functional groups on the surface where composition-derived bonds exist before plasma irradiation. Plasma irradiation has been reported to not only activate functional groups but also introduce gas elements into the target. The introduction of O facilitates the formation of SiO2. N2 plasma contributes on the formation of Si–N bonds and more C-related bonds. Thus, the preirradiation state is likely to remain because of the use of N2, which is more inert than O2. − More specifically, the introduction of nitrogen by N2 plasma increases Si–N bonds, and O2 plasma increases Si–O bonds. Furthermore, the inert nature of N2 plasma compared to the strong etching and oxidizing effects of O2 plasma is expected to result in milder surface alteration and maintain the carbon bonds in the film without removing them. As a result, N2 plasma creates a surface with more carbon-related surface bonds than O2 plasma.

Figure a shows the amount of dangling bonds in the SiCN-B film before and after the plasma treatments. The amount of dangling bonds per unit area increases similarly after irradiation with O2 and N2 plasma. Thus, the overall increase in the amount of active functional groups does not depend on the plasma species. In view of the XPS results in Figure , we reasonably assumed that a larger proportion of the total active functional groups is N- and C-related in the case of N2-plasma-treated SiCN-B than in the case of O2-plasma-treated SiCN-B. From this viewpoint, even if the total amount of dangling bonds after each plasma treatment is the same, the proportion of the functional groups responsible for the dangling bonds may change. Thus, differences in composition can occur in the region of a few nanometers of plasma action even if the overall composition of the film does not change. This measurement could not take care of Queue time, so this compares the change on the amount of dangling bonds in the film depending on the plasma conditions.

The results of PAS measurements of the atomic vacancy defects in SiCN films before and after N2 plasma irradiation are shown in Figure b. In general, a higher S-value indicates larger or more abundant atomic vacancy defects in a film. , In addition, a factor in the case of low S-values is the impurities such as water filling the defects. Figure b shows that, before plasma irradiation, SiCN-B had a larger S-value than SiCN-A in the topmost few nanometers of the surface and a smaller S-value in the bulk region. After plasma irradiation, the relationship remained unchanged, although N2 plasma irradiation resulted in smaller S-values in the subsurface region. The S value of the topmost surface is lowered by the plasma because the active dangling bonds formed on the surface tend to cause annihilation in the middle of the road, and the annihilation under the topmost surface is easily reduced as same as the previous report. Therefore, the defects are considered to increase at the subsurface after plasma activation.

The aforementioned results indicate that the SiCN-B film contains more atomic defects than the SiCN-A film starting from the deposition step. The TDS measurements show that SiCN-A exhibits almost no water desorption below 250 °C, whereas SiCN-B shows substantial water desorption. On the basis of these results, we speculated that the bulk region contains a large amount of water, which is why fewer defects are detected in the bulk region. On the topmost surface, defects are thought to appear because of the spontaneous desorption of water. This interpretation supports the existence of state differences in the topmost surface area, as noted in the discussion of the results in Figure . Si and C dangling bonds are known to be reactive toward water-based substances that normally exist in films of dielectrics such as SiO2 and SiCN. ,, Thus, C dangling bonds, which might be more abundant near the topmost surface in SiCN-B, may contribute to the dynamics that consume water at the bonding interface.

3.4. Analysis of Bonding Interface

To clarify the interfacial water consumption mechanism of SiCN-B, we analyzed the bonding interface. Figure shows the results of PAS measurements of the bonding interface. The S value of SiCN-A rises to the bulk S value as a monolayer film and then gradually decreases under the influence of the Si substrate. The overall S value of SiCN-A is larger than that of SiCN-B at the same PBA temperature, which is consistent with the results of surface PAS analysis in Figure b. On the other hand, the interfacial S-value of SiCN-A is higher than SiCN-B at PBA 250 °C, even if the interface is formed by the top-surface with less vacancies from surface PAS measurement. In other words, the interfacial S-value of SiCN-B is lower than SiCN-A, possibly due to its potential of much dangling bonds from the top-surface of SiCN-B. Regarding SiCN-B, there is a relatively steep upward trend toward the interface at 200 °C, but the trend becomes more gradual with increasing temperature. The overall value increases with annealing at higher temperatures, which is consistently attributed to diffusion and desorption of water or impurities from the bonding film.

The lower S-value indicates that fewer vacancies are present. , In view of Figure , the lower S value at the SiCN-B interface relative to SiCN-A can be attributed to the reduction of defects at the interface due to bond formation and water consumption by dangling bonds. However, Figure b shows that dangling bonds at the topmost surface of SiCN-B are more abundant than those of SiCN-A. Therefore, there must be factors that facilitate interfacial bond formation and water consumption more than those of SiCN-A in the interface formation. It is possible that the proportion of dangling bond-bearing elements present in SiCN-B is not normal Si, but rather, for example, more C dangling bonds. This is not the elemental composition, but the proportion of elements that expose dangling bonds, which is not contractional with the results of ERDA. In the case of SiCN-B, a large amount of C dangling bonds and water molecules are thought to react to form a dense interfacial layer, which is evened more at higher temperatures such as 430 °C. On the other hand, the S value of the interface in SiCN-A is higher than that in SiCN-B, suggesting that the interface formation is not completed and that water byproducts are still generated. This is because the low S value on the subsurface in SiCN-A with N2 plasma from Figure b suggests that the water-consuming groups, represented by the carbon dangling bonds, are less likely to be present at the interface.

From these, we can say that dangling bonds are very functional in consuming water at the interface and that their type is important. However, it is difficult to define the type of dangling bonds and their states at this point, so they will be discussed in the subsequent interfacial analysis. Please note that the analysis concentrates on SiCN-B. Therefore, a detailed interface analysis for all conditions is necessary to make the same statements.

TEM and EELS results for the bonding interface of SiCN-B using O2 and N2 plasma are shown in Figure . Figure a shows that the bonding interface formed by O2 plasma consists solely of SiO. By contrast, the interface formed by N2 plasma consists of an SiCO layer a few nanometers thick between SiO layers. This SiCO layer is unique to this processing condition; it was not observed in a prior study. , Thus, this unique interface can be derived from SiCN-B film properties, such as surface composition or etc. Compared with the C−K EELS spectrum of the SiCN bulk (d = 0.00 nm) layer, a distinct shoulder is observed at ∼285 eV in the spectrum corresponding to the SiCO bonding interface (d = 15.78 nm). These regions exhibit a greater contribution from π*-bonding, whereas the bulk peak at ∼292 eV is from σ*-bonding. Furthermore, the presence of peaks at 285–288 eV can be observed, which are indicative of C–OH and C–O–C functional groups. Also, there is almost no spectrum at the SiO layer region (d = 14.28 nm), where C itself is not present, consistent with the results of Figure b. The EELS spectrum of the interface on SiCN-B with O2 plasma is not shown because the C–K peak did not appear due to the absence of carbon at the interface.

Given the results of the aforementioned analyses, the SiCO layer is formed at the interface when the SiCN-B-specific near-surface C dangling bonds are retained after the N2 plasma treatment and bonded together. Therefore, C dangling bonds can react with water at lower activation energies than Si dangling bonds. As illustrated in Figure b, the C–OH and C–O–C peaks at the SiCO interface are of particular significance, as they provide compelling evidence that the C dangling bond is instrumental in both the reaction with water and the formation of interfacial covalent bonds. On the other hand, there are other possibilities, such as C dangling bonds being not suitable for water but for H2. , This property may lead to the completion of interfacial bonding and water consumption at lower temperatures. In this case, Si dangling bonds may require higher temperatures to form an interface with the SiO-dominant top surface after the O2 plasma treatment. In addition, according to the EELS results from the reference C–K spectra, the structure of bulk SiCN is more like that of diamond, whereas the structure of the interface is similar to that of graphite carbon. This indicates that more dangling bonds would be present at a certain layer interface. Thus, these dangling bonds might function as active sites to consume water. In other words, not only C dangling bonds but also a unique layer interface characteristic was found to be one element governing the ability to consume remaining water around the bonding interface. Furthermore, π* bondings can be CC, etc., and it is thought that carbon bonding as well as conventional water-mediated bonding at the interface is relevant for interface formation. This may contribute to increased bonding strength at low temperatures and high resistance against internal water stress corrosion. In addition, interfacial water can diffuse to the film bulk region. SiCN-B-derived C dangling bonds remain around the interface after the O2 plasma treatment, which may require higher-temperature PBA to provide sufficient energy for water molecules to diffuse out of the interface and react with the C dangling bonds. To clarify these dynamics, a deeper investigation of the role of each type of dangling bond at different depths from the wafer bonding interface is critical.

To further analyze the interface bonding state, we conducted postdebonding XPS measurements (Figure ). Delamination was carried out in a glovebox, and the sample was transferred to the XPS instrument using a transfer vessel to avoid exposing the revealed interface to air. The bonding in samples treated with N2 plasma is still related to carbon, whereas SiO2 accounts for a large proportion of the bonding in the samples treated with O2 plasma. The samples treated with N2 plasma also contain many nitrogen-derived Si–N bonds.

On the basis of the aforementioned results, SiO, SiN, and SiC (C)-mediated bonds may exist at the SiCN-B bonded interface in the N2 plasma-treated samples. In addition, more of the CO bonds are likely to be C–O–C bonds formed by the C dangling bonds consuming a large amount of water. These results support the previous results related to the interfacial water consumption mechanism associated with the combination of SiCN-B and a N2 plasma treatment. Another possibility is that the C–C bonds may have been broken, resulting in the appearance of CO bonds. Moreover, the presence of a small number of Si–N bonds may be due to the introduction of N by the N2 plasma; N2 is more inert than O2 and therefore less likely to be involved in interfacial bonding. However, further investigations are needed to clarify the effect of N-related bonding.

A schematic of the bonding mechanism deduced from the results of this study is shown in Figure . In SiCN-B, subsurface C dangling bonds are retained after the N2 plasma treatment. After this bonding process, the SiCO layer is formed by interfacial bonding and water consumption during annealing at low temperatures. By this mechanism, the maximum bonding strength is achieved at lower temperatures and the interfacial water is consumed. Finally, the mechanism that achieves a highly reliable bonding interface without bonding strength decay has been clarified. However, a detailed analysis of the reproducibility of these formed interfacial layers and the bonding interfaces of other films is essential to verify the same statements.

4. Conclusions

In summary, plasma-activated wafer bonding with SiCN was carried out for high-quality bonding with low-temperature annealing. Compared with the SiCN-A film, the SiCN-B film with a high surface defect density exhibited higher bonding strength and superior interfacial water consumption at lower temperatures. We also found that, compared with O2 plasma, N2 plasma produced a more ideal surface and interface state with more C dangling bonds. This result suggests that the functional groups related to the C dangling bonds react with a large amount of water and form covalent bonds at the bonding interface at low temperatures to create a dense SiCO layer. In the future, the formation process and reproducibility of this interfacial layer should be studied in greater detail, along with other processing conditions.
