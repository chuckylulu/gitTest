Abstract

Wafer bonding is
a step in processing of state-of-the-art integration
architectures in CMOS devices. Sufficiently high bonding strength
and low distortion with high alignment accuracy are essential to realize
these device structures. A challenge in realizing advanced architectures
is reducing the thermal history associated with the bonding process.
Although much research has been conducted on wafer bonding methods
compatible with the latest semiconductor manufacturing processes,
discussions on the interface mechanisms during low temperature annealing
have been insufficient. In this study, plasma-activated bonding was
carried out using SiCN, which is a major bonding dielectric material.
The bonding strength and water remaining at the interface were subsequently
evaluated. We found that a SiCN film achieved greater bonding strength
after post bond annealing at a low temperature of 250 °C and
completely consumed the interfacial water. Analyses of the surface
and interface revealed the carbon bonding leads to great bonding interface
by low-temperature annealing.

1. Introduction

Advanced 3D integration
structures such as complementary field-effect
transistors (CFETs) and backside power delivery networks (BSPDNs)
have been progressively investigated to achieve greater integrity
and lower power consumption per unit area in advanced processors.

−

 Direct wafer bonding is essential for these architectures, and minimizing
distortion at the bonding step is important to ensure overlay accuracy
in lithography during backside fabrication. Thus, an atomic-level
understanding of direct bonding technology is essential for the advanced-node
complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS) process era. In addition,
hybrid bonding plays an important role in 3D chiplet applications
that require high-density connections between wafers or chips, such
as memory stacking and sensor integration.

,

 In
such applications, highly reliable bonding with extreme grid alignment
and sufficient bonding strength is essential to maintain high yields,
where high thermal, electrical, and mechanical reliability are required
for the bonding dielectric film. In addition, the thermal history
resulting from high temperatures during bonding processes is becoming
a serious issue in maintaining device performance.

,

 Therefore,
achieving high bonding strength and reliable interfaces at low temperatures
is the key to the further development of 3D structures fabricated
using bonding processes.

SiO
2
 deposited by chemical
vapor deposition (CVD) has
been mainly used in the direct bonding process for CMOS processes.
However, the strength of the resultant bond is insufficient when postbonding
annealing (PBA) is conducted at a temperature of less than 250 °C.
In addition, CVD-SiO
2
 has excess water at the interface,
resulting in the formation of microvoids, which adversely affect the
strength of bonds formed in low-temperature processes. Overcoming this problem requires the development of new
materials that form a water-free interface that can maintain stable
bonding strength after low-temperature annealing.

Recently,
CVD-SiCN has attracted attention as an alternative material
for achieving low-temperature bonds with the desired properties. CVD-SiCN
has been shown to maintain high bonding strength after low-temperature
annealing and to exhibit superior void suppression capability compared
with conventional CVD-SiO
2
.

−

 SiCN is also known to
function as a capping film against Cu in the back-end of line processes. These properties make CVD-SiCN a promising option
for improving the reliability of devices fabricated using low-temperature
bonding processes. However, there are many unexplored aspects of the
mechanism involving SiCN, especially the specific influence of plasma
activation conditions on the bonding strength. Previous studies have
partially clarified the effects of dangling bonds and surface activation
at the SiCN bonding interface.

−

 However, the full extent of the
factors contributing to the strength enhancement at low temperatures
has not been elucidated, nor has the role of interfacial water.

In the present study, the mechanism resulting in a high bonding
strength and a water-free interface in the low-temperature bonding
of SiCN is clarified. From these findings, process factors that result
in a more reliable bonding interface are proposed. Specifically, the
conditions that promote the formation of an ideal interface are identified
through a detailed evaluation of the changes in bonding strength under
various plasma activation conditions. The aim is to provide, as a
contribution to the further development of advanced integration technology,
new knowledge for improving the reliability of low-temperature bonding
processes using plasma activation.
2. Experimental
Section

SiCN films were deposited onto 300 mm Si wafers via
CVD, achieving
a target thickness of 100 nm. Each wafer was subjected to chemical
mechanical polishing (CMP) to obtain a surface roughness less than
Rq 0.15 nm and the 80 nm film thickness, where the deposition parameters
were adjusted to produce specific film properties. After CMP, N
2
 or O
2
 plasma activation and a deionized water
rinse (hydration) were conducted. Finally, wafer bonding was performed.
PBA was performed under nitrogen ambient at 200 °C, 250 °C,
or 300 °C for 2 h.

Composition analysis was carried out
with Rutherford backscattering
spectrometry with elastic recoil detection analysis (RBS–ERDA,
HRBS500, Kobelco). The refractive indexes of SiCN films were measured
by ellipsometry (RC2X-XNIR, J.A. Woollam). Gas emissions from the films were analyzed using thermal desorption
spectroscopy (TDS, TDS1200II, ESCO).

,

The
double cantilever beam (DCB) test was conducted using a custom-designed
apparatus to assess bonding strength. In this setup, an actuator-driven
blade is introduced into the bonding interface with an adjustable
insertion distance and speed to precisely initiate delamination. This
semiautomated approach enables consistent and reproducible measurements
of bonding strength. The bonding energy

The behavior of water stress corrosion manifests as

Chemical

ARM200F, JEOL). In addition, after debonding,
XPS analysis was performed to characterize interfacial bonds.

dangling bonds are retained after the N
2
 plasma treatment
and bonded together. Therefore, C dangling bonds can react with water
at lower activation energies than Si dangling bonds. As illustrated in 
Figure 

b, the C–OH and C–O–C peaks at
the SiCO interface are of particular significance, as they provide
compelling evidence that the C dangling bond is instrumental in both
the reaction with water and the formation of interfacial covalent
bonds. On the other hand, there are other possibilities, such as C
dangling bonds being not suitable for water but for H
2
.

,

 This property may lead to the completion of interfacial bonding
and water consumption at lower temperatures. In this case, Si dangling
bonds may require higher temperatures to form an interface with the
SiO-dominant top surface after the O
2
 plasma treatment.
In addition, according to the EELS results from the reference C–K
spectra, the structure of bulk SiCN is more like that of diamond,
whereas the structure of the interface is similar to that of graphite
carbon. This indicates that more dangling
bonds would be present at a certain layer interface. Thus, these dangling
bonds might function as active sites to consume water. In other words,
not only C dangling bonds but also a unique layer interface characteristic
was found to be one element governing the ability to consume remaining
water around the bonding interface. Furthermore, π* bondings
can be CC, etc., and it is thought that carbon bonding as
well as conventional water-mediated bonding at the interface is relevant
for interface formation. This may contribute to increased bonding
strength at low temperatures and high resistance against internal
water stress corrosion. In addition, interfacial water can diffuse
to the film bulk region. SiCN-B-derived
C dangling bonds remain around the interface after the O
2
 plasma treatment, which may require higher-temperature PBA to provide
sufficient energy for water molecules to diffuse out of the interface
and react with the C dangling bonds. To clarify these dynamics, a
deeper investigation of the role of each type of dangling bond at
different depths from the wafer bonding interface is critical.

To further analyze the interface bonding state, we conducted postdebonding
XPS measurements (
Figure 

). Delamination was carried out in a glovebox, and the sample
was transferred to the XPS instrument using a transfer vessel to avoid
exposing the revealed interface to air. The bonding in samples treated
with N
2
 plasma is still related to carbon, whereas SiO
2
 accounts for a large proportion of the bonding in the samples
treated with O
2
 plasma. The samples treated with N
2
 plasma also contain many nitrogen-derived Si–N bonds.

On the basis of the aforementioned results, SiO,

to be C–O–C bonds formed by the C dangling bonds consuming
a large amount of water. These results support the previous results
related to the interfacial water consumption mechanism associated
with the combination of SiCN-B and a N
2
 plasma treatment.
Another possibility is that the C–C bonds may have been broken,
resulting in the appearance of CO bonds. Moreover, the presence of
a small number of Si–N bonds may be due to the introduction
of N by the N
2
 plasma; N
2
 is more inert than
O
2
 and therefore less likely to be involved in interfacial
bonding. However, further investigations are needed to clarify the
effect of N-related bonding.

A schematic of the bonding mechanism
deduced from the results of
this study is shown in 
Figure 

. In SiCN-B, subsurface C dangling bonds are retained after
the N
2
 plasma treatment. After this bonding process, the
SiCO layer is formed by interfacial bonding and water consumption
during annealing at low temperatures. By this mechanism, the maximum
bonding strength is achieved at lower temperatures and the interfacial
water is consumed. Finally, the mechanism that achieves a highly reliable
bonding interface without bonding strength decay has been clarified.
However, a detailed analysis of the reproducibility of these formed
interfacial layers and the bonding interfaces of other films is essential
to verify the same statements.

4. Conclusions

In summary, plasma-activated
wafer bonding with SiCN was carried
out for high-quality bonding with low-temperature annealing. Compared
with the SiCN-A film, the SiCN-B film with a high surface defect density
exhibited higher bonding strength and superior interfacial water consumption
at lower temperatures. We also found that, compared with O
2
 plasma, N
2
 plasma produced a more ideal surface and interface
state with more C dangling bonds. This result suggests that the functional
groups related to the C dangling bonds react with a large amount of
water and form covalent bonds at the bonding interface at low temperatures
to create a dense SiCO layer. In the future, the formation process
and reproducibility of this interfacial layer should be studied in
greater detail, along with other processing conditions.
