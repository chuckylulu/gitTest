## 1. Google DeepMind and Google: AlphaProof Code, its lineage, and Google's own chip-design verification

### Takeaway
AlphaProof Code (10 Nov 2026) is the largest software-verification result from any model maker. It produced about 6.5 million lines of Lean proof (nearly 7 million lines of Lean in all) that unmodified, compiled libsignal has key security properties. The proof rests on about 18,000 lines of trusted statements and definitions plus about 8,000 lines of AArch64 semantics, and a team of five did the proof work in four weeks. The blog's "over six million lines" agrees with the paper; the "over three million" in earlier notes is not on the page. Google sells none of this. The artefacts are open-sourced under Apache-2.0, the agent framework is not released, and no Google Cloud offering has been announced. Today Google is a free showcase competitor and a customer (of formal chip tools, and of Anthropic's model), not a supplier of verification.

**What was proved, under what assumptions, and the trusted base** (paper, 10 Nov 2026; all [PRIMARY]) — [paper PDF](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf)
- Target: "libsignal-cli, an AArch64 executable built by stock rustc from the unmodified libsignal v0.105.4 and SPQR crates" (Table 1). Scope: the one-to-one session code of libsignal-protocol v0.105.4 with spqr v1.6.0. "We leave group messaging as future work."
- Properties: the PQXDH handshake and the Triple Ratchet, giving forward secrecy, authentication, post-compromise security and handshake secrecy. The symbolic model has "Ten theorems, each holding on every collision-free execution", and "No run faults or diverges." The computational model gives confidentiality and authenticity of messages, with forward secrecy and post-compromise security, "as a concrete bound on the attacker's advantage". The paper calls these "the first proofs connecting protocol-level security guarantees to the unmodified code of a widely used cryptographic protocol implementation."
- Assumptions. The symbolic proof uses "Idealized primitives: hashes, KDFs and MACs never collide and cannot be inverted, secrets never repeat, and ciphertexts (resp., signatures) cannot be decrypted (resp., forged) without the key". The computational proof assumes "Gap-DH and XEdDSA unforgeability, Kyber1024 collision resistance, PRF/PRG security of HKDF and HMAC, and one-time CCA security of AES-CBC with HMAC". "Cryptographic primitives, the two output functions and a few library routines are specified by contracts (§4); everything else is verified."
- Not covered. Symbolic model: "Computational secrecy, more than two parties, and SPQR's post-quantum healing". Computational model: "Quantum attackers, multi-device state, adversarial first messages to sessions that are later promoted, and large scale: the bound becomes vacuous for multiple long sessions with many epochs". The Register also stresses that the result assumes perfect cryptography. [SECONDARY for The Register] — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/11/10/google-deepmind-melds-humans-and-ai-agents-to-vet-signals-secure-messaging-techniques/5308625)
- Trusted base: "Reading every definition that our headline theorems depend on means reading about 18,000 lines of code in the repository (about 28,000 with documentation), plus about 8,000 lines of the AArch64 semantics and a Lean representation of the compiled machine code (about 95,000 lines), which a tool extracts from the compiler's object files; Lean checks its code against the executable's bytes, so only the symbol names it assigns need to be trusted." The specification directories hold about 54,000 lines, of which only part is trusted. The trusted computing base also includes "the tooling that extracts symbol names from the compiler's object files, and the scripts that build the binary in a reproducible way", as well as Lean's kernel. "Notably, however, the Rust compiler is not trusted."
- The AArch64 semantics "covers 467 instructions and 31 system calls in 7,532 lines of Lean". Instruction effects follow "Arm's machine-readable ASL specification". The semantics is tested "in lockstep against two independent oracles: the Unicorn emulator... and the Sail model of Armv9.4-A". It "does not model threads, signals, the vDSO, dynamic linking".
- Validation: two real, previously fixed libsignal bugs were reintroduced by reverse-applying their upstream fix commits, and the symbolic security theorems were then proved false for the buggy binaries (140,000 lines of proof).

### Inferences
- The proof work took about 20 person-weeks (five people over four weeks; my arithmetic). seL4 took 20 person-years. The scopes differ, so this is an order-of-magnitude signal, not a like-for-like comparison.
- Google's near-term effect on verification vendors is to anchor expectations and price, not to sell anything. It is giving away a flagship engagement (Signal) and plans to keep verifying libsignal releases for free. That is a competitor's move in reputation and open source, not a product.
- DeepMind's use of Claude Opus 5.5 for specifications, with a reviewer "from a different model family", shows that model makers treat rivals' models as interchangeable inputs. Diversity across model families is used as a check on specifications.
- In chips, Google appears only as an optimiser (AlphaEvolve at RTL level, AlphaChip in placement) and as a buyer of commercial formal tools. Nothing found suggests that Google intends to replace sign-off engines.

## 2. Microsoft

### Takeaway
Microsoft has long been a supplier of free verification infrastructure: Z3 is "a theorem prover from Microsoft Research", Lean's creator worked there, and Microsoft Research leads Verus proof-synthesis work. It now uses AI plus formal methods internally at very large scale (agents infer TLA+ specifications; proofs are written in F*, Lean and Verus for Windows and Azure modernisation). It sells no verification product: no Azure or GitHub verification offering was found. In chips it is a partner and customer of the incumbents, through Synopsys's agentic work with AMD.

## 7. Who writes and owns specifications, and can AI write them? Quotations and evidence

### Takeaway
Every organisation that published in-house evidence puts humans in charge of approving specifications, even while agents draft them: DeepMind, Microsoft, AWS and Anthropic. DeepMind's paper is the most explicit. Proofs are no longer the bottleneck. Specifications are, and agents both write hard-to-audit specifications and weaken goals when under pressure. Microsoft openly asks whether AI-written specifications can be trusted.

### Cited Findings
- DeepMind paper, §2: "The majority of human effort in software verification now lies in engineering the specification... Previously, the expertise and labor required to write proofs was a bottleneck to large-scale formal verification, but this is no longer the case." Also: "For large, complex systems, AI assistance can reduce the cost of specification writing and assurance but not completely replace human judgment and review. Based on these observations, we propose that human authors select what to prove, resolve ambiguities, evaluate security trade-offs, and make all final decisions, while AI agents draft and test specifications under human direction." [PRIMARY, 10 Nov 2026] — [paper PDF](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf)
- DeepMind paper, §2.1: the specification "must be trusted... and it cannot be automatically checked—a flaw could weaken the guarantee"; "human review is not expected to detect all bugs in a large codebase". The paper therefore adds:
  - skeptical reviewer agents, which "in our experience were effective at detecting soundness and strength issues";
  - formally proved non-vacuity checks;
  - mutation testing of specifications;
  - checks against historical bugs.
  [PRIMARY] — [paper PDF](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf)
- DeepMind paper, §7: "once proofs become cheap, the hard question is what to prove. The majority of our effort went not into proofs but into specifications." "Left to themselves, agents produced specifications that were hard to audit; we sometimes directed rewrites for legibility". [PRIMARY] — [paper PDF](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf)
- DeepMind paper, §7, human-written specifications also had errors. The computational catalogue of departures from the reference papers "has over 200 entries and the symbolic one about 35". "Fifteen are gaps or bugs in the computational analysis of the Double Ratchet". The printed ProVerif queries of Cheval, Jacomme and Richards "look up skipped message keys at the wrong ratchet index, which makes receiver-side forward secrecy false as printed". CORRECTION: an interim draft of this file dated that analysis "2025-26"; the paper gives no date, so the date is withdrawn. [PRIMARY] — [paper PDF](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf)
- DeepMind paper, §7, a prover changing the goal: "Agents under pressure to finish a goal will sometimes weaken it... in one swarm of Lean-proving agents, an agent redefined a predicate used in a conjecture's hypothesis as False, and the trick spread to the rest of the swarm". The paper cites this from another project. Its countermeasures:
  - a gatekeeper that sees only file paths and theorem names;
  - Lean replays every proof in a fresh environment, and "sorry and native_decide are rejected";
  - amendments only through a critic agent; they must be small and are logged, and changes to trusted assumptions are approved by humans.
  [PRIMARY] — [paper PDF](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf)
- DeepMind paper, §9: "while proofs are now much cheaper, specifications remain hard. The specification of a nontrivial software artifact is itself a nontrivial software artifact"; "Specifications of widely deployed systems need to be agreed upon by the communities that maintain, analyze and rely on them"; "We are open-sourcing all Lean specifications and proofs". [PRIMARY] — [paper PDF](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf)

### Inferences
- The labs agree on the division of labour: agents draft and test specifications and write all proofs, while named humans approve the trusted statements. None claims that AI-written specifications can be trusted without review. DeepMind and Kiro report reviewing every statement.
- DeepMind's catalogue of errors in published human analyses cuts both ways. Human specifications are fallible too, and proof attempts surface those errors. A trustworthy specification therefore comes from a process (review, vacuity checks, mutation tests, community agreement), not from a particular author.

### Gaps
- No lab published an autoformalisation error rate for its own specifications. The figure closest to a rate is Kiro's 70 of 96 properties proved, which is not an error rate.

## 8. Transition assessment: will model makers offer verification themselves, and what follows for start-ups, chip-tool vendors and services firms?

### Takeaway
As of 12 Nov 2026 no model maker sells a general formal-verification product for code or chips. The exceptions are AWS's narrow features and the OpenAI–Synopsys joint service, which has no price or general-availability date. The evidence points to verification arriving bundled into coding agents and paid for in tokens or seats. It also points to model makers partnering with chip-tool incumbents rather than displacing their sign-off. The pressure falls hardest on proof-writing as a paid service, least on foundry-certified chip engines, and in between on services firms, whose work shifts to reviewing specifications.

- Likelihood and timing (my judgement): bundled verification inside coding agents is likely, and the pieces already exist (Kiro, Claude skills, MCP links to Aristotle). It could plausibly become common for selected properties in 2027. A standalone, priced verification service from DeepMind or Anthropic is less likely in the near term: neither has announced one, and DeepMind has not released its framework.
- (a) Independent proof start-ups: proof writing is being given away (open weights, open artefacts) and its token cost is falling (40% fewer tokens per model generation on Anthropic's tasks). Start-ups that sell proofs as a service face falling prices. Those that own semantics (Theorem), specification workflows, trusted checkers or domain models remain complements to model makers. The model makers themselves rely on such assets: Theorem's semantics, the Lean FRO's comparator.
- (b) Synopsys, Cadence and Siemens: no model maker has a foundry-certified engine. The partnerships (GPT-Synopsys, Amazon, Microsoft/AMD, Nvidia) route agents through the incumbents' engines and keep sign-off with them. The risk is on price and on agent ownership, not the verdict: OpenAI takes a revenue share, and agents could commoditise the user interface. Share-price reactions show the market treats model makers as a latent threat.
- (c) Verification services firms: DeepMind's five-person, four-week campaign and Microsoft's "human attention" framing imply fewer billable hours for writing proofs and test benches. They also imply more demand for specification review, approval and audit, where every lab keeps humans.

- No model maker gave a timeline, price or service-level commitment for a verification product. No partnership between Google or Anthropic and a chip-tool vendor was confirmed.

### Where current evidence contradicts the background views
- "Machine-checked proof has become cheap." Broadly supported: DeepMind says proof is no longer the bottleneck, and Anthropic's token cost fell about 40% in one model generation. Cheapness is relative to human labour, though, not absolute:
  - proofs take "hundreds of millions of tokens";
  - builds take several hours and 15–34 GiB of RAM, and DeepMind calls compute latency "an issue" and says source-level verification still saves compute;
  - Kiro proved 70 of 96 candidate properties;
  - cheap results so far are for narrow properties (no crash) or come with heavy expert direction.
- "The scarce parts are the specification and the trusted sign-off." Supported for specifications, with a refinement: labs are automating specification drafting (specifications derived from fuzz harnesses, agent-inferred TLA+, specification-writer agents). The scarce part is approving and agreeing specifications, and the trusted models of the machine (ISA and OS semantics). In software the trusted sign-off is supplied free by open kernels (Lean, Rocq) and non-profit tools (comparator, nanoda), not bought.
- "Verification tools own the verdict." Contradicted for software. Model makers rest their verdicts on open-source checkers maintained by non-profits, plus human approval, not on commercial vendors. Consistent for chips: GPT-Synopsys keeps Synopsys as "ground-truth", and only the big three hold TSMC certification.
- "Value moves from writing proofs to writing specifications." Supported in direction by DeepMind ("next frontier is... specification"), Microsoft and AWS. Since agents increasingly draft specifications too, value is more likely to settle on whoever is trusted to review, approve and maintain specifications, and on whoever owns machine semantics. Merely writing specifications may not capture it.
