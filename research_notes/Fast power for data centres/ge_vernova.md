## 1. Products and stage

### Takeaway
GE Vernova sells the full gas-turbine range relevant to data-centre power — trailer-mounted TM2500 and LM2500XPRESS aeroderivatives (~35 MW, 5-minute starts) used as bridge/on-site power, up to 9HA.02 (571 MW simple cycle) for base-load combined cycle — plus grid-connection equipment (transformers via the now wholly owned Prolec GE, switchgear, breakers, HVDC, synchronous condensers). Data-centre-specific electrical products inside the facility (MV-UPS, solid-state transformers) are new and pre-commercial: first MV-UPS shipments are slated for mid-2027 and the first SST unit was in testing in 2026. BWRX-300 SMRs are a 2030s item (first unit under construction in Ontario; first US construction permit issued September 2026).

### Inferences
- For "fast power", GE Vernova's own numbers split the product line into two speed tiers: aeroderivatives (minutes to start, ~1.5–6 months ship-to-COD) and heavy-duty F/HA units (~18–24 months ship-to-COD and multi-year order lead times — see section 6). The data-centre "speed" claim therefore depends on which tier is meant.
- All efficiency/heat-rate figures above are vendor ISO ratings (15 °C, sea level, new and clean, LHV); site output and efficiency at hot Texas or Middle East sites will be lower (GE's own pages flag ambient dependence, but publish no derating curves).

## 2. Technology and roadmap with dates

### Takeaway
The roadmap that matters for data centres is a manufacturing roadmap, not a new turbine model: heavy-duty output to ~20 GW/yr from mid-2026 (reached in Q3 2026), ~24 GW in 2028 and 30 GW in 2030, plus a doubling of transformer and switchgear output 2024–2028. Product innovation for data centres is concentrated in power electronics (MV-UPS mid-2027, SST) and grid components (Trench).

## 9. Science and engineering

### Takeaway
The underlying technology is mature: GE Vernova reports 128 HA units operating with >4 million hours, LM6000 ~60 million and LM2500 ~140 million fleet hours. Demonstrated capabilities (5-minute aero starts, 60–88 MW/min HA ramps, ~42–44% simple-cycle and ~63–64% combined-cycle LHV efficiency) are vendor-rated at ISO conditions; third-party verification found in this session is limited to the HA blade-oxidation disclosure (a 2018 problem GE took a charge for) and indirect supplier data. Unproven items: hydrogen above 50% in HA turbines and 100% in aeros outside GEV's own test cells, ammonia firing, SST/MV-UPS at data-centre scale, and turbines following sub-second AI-training load swings without buffering — GE Vernova's own MV-UPS launch implies turbines need protection from such swings. The binding physical constraint is the supply of hot-section castings and forgings.

### Cited Findings
**Demonstrated (company-reported)**
- HA fleet: >4 million commercial operating hours, 128 units operating in 21 countries, ~74 GW of plant capacity — [GE Vernova release, 26 May 2026, company](https://www.gevernova.com/news/press-releases/ge-vernova-ha-gas-turbine-fleet-surpasses-4-million); 100th HA commissioned and >53 GW of H-class installed by mid-2024 — [Q2 2024 release, 24 Jul 2024, company](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1996810/000199681024000065/gev2q2024earningsrelease.htm)

### Inferences
- If EIA's 7,535 Btu/kWh is on the usual HHV basis, it equals ~45% HHV (~50% LHV) for the average US combined-cycle plant, versus GEV's ~5,300 Btu/kWh (≈64% LHV, new-and-clean ISO) for a 7HA.03 2x1 — i.e., new HA plants are rated ~20–25% more fuel-efficient than the fleet average, but plant-level field heat rates for HA units were not checked (researcher calculation; basis assumption flagged).
- 50% hydrogen by volume corresponds to only roughly a quarter of fuel energy (hydrogen's volumetric heating value is about one-third of methane's), so CO2 reduction at the demonstrated HA blend limit is modest (physics-based estimate, not a GEV figure).
- Solid ground? On the evidence gathered: aeroderivative and F-class technology is long-proven by fleet hours; HA technology is commercially established (128 units, >4M hours) after an early, disclosed blade-oxidation issue; the unproven parts are fuel transition (high-H2, ammonia), new power-electronics products (MV-UPS, SST) and direct load-following of AI clusters. The main engineering risk to "fast power" is manufacturing throughput (castings/forgings, factory ramp), not turbine physics.
