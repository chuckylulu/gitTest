## 1. What does the sodium-cooled fast reactor (SFR) operating record show (EBR-I, EBR-II, FFTF, Fermi-1, Phénix, Superphénix, Monju, BN-350/600/800, PFBR, CFR-600)?

### Takeaway
Small test SFRs (EBR-II, FFTF) worked well and EBR-II showed passive shutdown in 1986 tests. Most prototype and commercial-size SFRs had long outages, sodium leaks and fires, and very low lifetime load factors. Superphénix was under 7%, PFR 20.6% and Monju close to zero. Russia's BN-600 and BN-800 are the exceptions, with cumulative load factors of about 66–74%, though BN-600 had 27 sodium leaks and 14 fires by 1997. India's PFBR first reached criticality in April 2026, and China's CFR-600 has published almost no operating data.

### Cited Findings
**US experimental and demonstration plants**
- EBR-I (1.2 MWt / 0.2 MWe, sodium-potassium cooled, 94%-enriched uranium) went critical on 20 Dec 1951 and was the first reactor to generate electricity. It was designed with a prompt positive power coefficient and suffered a partial (40–50%) core meltdown on 29 Nov 1955 during an instability experiment. It ran until 30 Dec 1963. — [IPFM (Cochran, Feiveson, Patterson, Pshakin, Ramana, Schneider, Suzuki, von Hippel), "Fast Breeder Reactor Programs: History and Status," Research Report 8, Feb 2010, ch. 7](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
- EBR-II was a 62.5 MWt / 20 MWe pool-type SFR. It reached criticality with sodium on 11 Nov 1963 and design power on 25 Sep 1969, and ran initially on metallic HEU fuel. An adjoining Fuel Cycle Facility reprocessed and refabricated its fuel from 1964 to 1969. It became an irradiation facility in 1967 and began shutdown in Sep 1994 after 30 years. IPFM calls it "arguably the most successful of the U.S. fast reactors." — [IPFM 2010, ch. 7 p. 93](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
- On 3 Apr 1986, EBR-II ran an unprotected loss-of-flow test and a loss-of-heat-sink-without-scram test, both from full power. In both, the reactor shut itself down through natural processes, mainly thermal expansion. Pump coastdown was critical to the peak transient temperature, and later analysis found no perceptible fuel damage. — [Chang, Feldman & Betten (ANL), OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/5428837); ["Results and implications of the EBR-II inherent safety demonstration tests," OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/6770302) [IND-LAB]
- Fermi-1 (200 MWt / 66 MWe, loop design, HEU) went critical on 23 Aug 1963. In Oct 1966 a zirconium plate came loose and blocked sodium flow, and two of 105 fuel assemblies melted. Repairs took about four years, and a sodium explosion delayed the 1970 restart. In 1971 the plant generated 19.4 GWh, a 3.4% capacity factor. It shut down on 22 Sep 1972. — [IPFM 2010, ch. 7](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
- FFTF (400 MWt, loop-type, MOX, Hanford) reached criticality in 1980 and served as a test facility from 1982 to Apr 1992. Its tests included passive decay-heat removal by natural sodium convection. Deactivation was decided in Dec 1993. — [IPFM 2010, ch. 7](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]

**France, UK and Japan**
- **Phénix (250 MWe)**
  - Cumulative load factor was 60.5% as of 1988. After unexplained reactivity transients, the load factor "plunged virtually to zero," and the plant was mostly shut down from 1991 to 1994.
  - About €600M was spent on upgrades between 1997 and 2003. It restarted in 2003 at 130 MWe net, reached a cumulative load factor of 44.6% by end-2007, and closed in 2009.
  - Secondary sodium leaks occurred in 1974–75, followed by sodium fires in 1976. Between Apr 1982 and Mar 1983 there were at least four sodium-water reaction incidents in the reheaters, affecting all three steam generators.
  - — [IPFM 2010, ch. 2](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
  - The same report's Table 3.4, citing PRIS, gives a different Phénix cumulative load factor of 33.72%. — [IPFM 2010, ch. 3](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
- **Superphénix (1,200 MWe net)**
  - It went critical on 7 Sep 1985, connected to the grid on 14 Jan 1986 and produced its last kWh on 24 Dec 1996. It never ran more than 17 months in a row.
  - It generated 8.2 TWh gross in total, giving a "lifetime load factor ... less than 7 percent" (6.6% in PRIS Table 3.4).
  - In May 1987 a sodium leak in the fuel-storage drum could not be repaired. Developing a new fuel-handling method took 10 months.
  - Construction cost rose 80% to FRF₁₉₈₅ 26 bn ($₂₀₀₈ 9.5 bn). The CEA put the investment cost per kW at 2.58 times that of a PWR. The total cost was about FRF 65 bn (NERSA).
  - — [IPFM 2010, ch. 2](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
- PFR (UK) had a cumulative load factor of 20.57%, BN-600 71.51% and Superphénix 6.6%, from IAEA PRIS as tabulated in 2010. — [IPFM 2010, Table 3.4](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
- **Monju (280 MWe)**
  - It went critical in 1994. On 8 Dec 1995, intense vibration caused the failure of a thermocouple attached to the secondary sodium loop, causing a sodium leak and fire "that melted the steel structures in the room." There were no injuries and no radioactive release. The operator's cover-up then delayed restart for years. — [IPFM 2010, ch. 4](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
  - In Dec 2016 Japan confirmed decommissioning, at about ¥375 bn (¥225 bn maintenance, ¥135 bn dismantling, ¥15 bn defuelling), to be completed by 2047. — [NucNet, "Japan confirms Monju decommissioning at cost of $3.2 billion," 22 Dec 2016](https://www.nucnet.org/all-the-news/2016/12/22/japan-confirms-monju-decommissioning-at-cost-of-3-2-billion) [IND-PRESS]
  - Reported sunk cost is about ¥1 trillion. Total operation was reportedly about 250 days. Reported leak size varies from 640 kg to about 700 kg. — [Wikipedia, "Monju Nuclear Power Plant"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monju_Nuclear_Power_Plant) [LOW]

**Russia/Kazakhstan**
- In late 1973 a steam-generator failure caused a major sodium fire at BN-350. The reactor was down for about four months and closed permanently in Apr 1999. — [IPFM 2010, ch. 5 p. 64](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
- BN-600 had 27 sodium leaks by 1997, 14 of which caused fires; the largest was 1,000 litres. Its steam generators are modular so they can be repaired at power. **India and China**
- India's 500 MWe PFBR at Kalpakkam reached first criticality at 8:25 pm on 6 Apr 2026. — [Dept. of Atomic Energy, press release, Apr 2026](https://dae.gov.in/prototype-fast-breeder-reactor-at-kalpakkam-tamil-nadu-attains-first-criticality) [STATE-OP]

**Physics and maintenance**
- Sodium boils at 883 °C, so the reactor runs at low pressure. However, sodium "reacts violently with water and burns if exposed to air." Steam generators "have proved to be one of the most troublesome features of breeder reactors." Na-24 activation (15-hour half-life) forces an intermediate sodium loop, which adds cost. — [IPFM 2010, Overview](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
- On void reactivity, IPFM writes that in a fast reactor "except for special core configurations, the reactivity will increase if the coolant is lost." — [IPFM 2010, Overview](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
- On in-service inspection and repair, IPFM writes that "During repairs, the fuel has to be removed, the sodium drained and the entire system flushed ... Such preparations can take months or years." Water reactors can instead be flooded and inspected with underwater periscopes and cameras. — [IPFM 2010, Overview](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]

### Inferences
- **Reliability.** Load factors for SFRs in market economies were 3–60%, against roughly 80% for LWRs in the IPFM comparison. These failures came mostly from:
  - sodium leaks and fires, at BN-350, BN-600, Phénix, Superphénix and Monju;
  - sodium-water reactions in steam generators, at BN-350 and Phénix;
  - slow repairs under opaque sodium, at Superphénix and Fermi-1.
- **Russian experience.** Russia's BN-600 and BN-800 show that a large pool SFR can sustain about 66–74% cumulative load factors with a mature operator and modular steam generators. That is still below the LWR fleet.
- **Passive shutdown.** The EBR-II 1986 tests are the strongest physical evidence for passive shutdown of a metal-fueled pool SFR. They were run at 62.5 MWt with HEU fuel, so extending them to larger HALEU cores (Natrium, Aurora) relies on analysis rather than test.
- **Design responses.** Natrium's design addresses three historical failure modes: a molten-salt intermediate loop instead of sodium-to-steam (avoiding sodium-water reactions), a guard vessel, and modular separation (see §3). Aurora's design details are not public enough to check the same way.
- **Economics.** The independent historical economic record (CEA ratio of 2.58 times PWR cost; Superphénix and Monju sunk costs) is unfavourable. That record is for plutonium breeders, not HALEU burners like Natrium and Aurora.

## 2. Metallic fuel: U-Zr and U-Pu-Zr experience, fuel forms planned for Natrium and Aurora, and qualification status

### Takeaway
Metallic U-Zr and U-Pu-Zr pins reached about 15–20 at% burnup in EBR-II and FFTF with few breaches. That depended on a low (75%) smear density to absorb swelling, and fuel-cladding chemical interaction (FCCI) limits performance at high temperature. Sodium-bonded spent fuel cannot go straight to a repository: DOE has spent decades treating about 3 t of it and expects to finish the EBR-II driver fuel in 2028. Natrium will start on sodium-bonded U-10Zr "Type 1" fuel, which closely follows EBR-II/FFTF pins. As of Sep 2026 NRC had given only "feedback" on TerraPower's fuel qualification topical report, and ACRS says fuel qualification will be completed before the operating-license review. Aurora's first core will be HALEU recovered from EBR-II fuel, and its fuel design has not had an NRC review.

### Cited Findings
**Irradiation record**
- U-Pu-Zr and U-Zr rods clad in 316, D9 or HT9 reached terminal burnups of "15 to >19 at.%" without breach. — [Pahl et al., "Irradiation Behavior of Metallic Fast Reactor Fuels" (PNNL-hosted)](https://www.pnnl.gov/sites/default/files/media/file/Irradiation%20Behavior%20of%20Metallic%20Fast%20Reactor%20Fuels.pdf) [IND-LAB/ACAD]
- HT9-clad endurance tests in EBR-II were "nearing the 20 at% burnup level without a single breach." In 1973 the smear density was lowered from 85% to 75%, so fuel first touches cladding only at about 33% radial swelling, which limits fuel-cladding mechanical interaction. — ["Metallic Fuels for Advanced Reactors" (PNNL-hosted)](https://www.pnnl.gov/sites/default/files/media/file/Metallic%20Fuels%20for%20Advanced%20Reactors.pdf) [IND-LAB/ACAD]
- In FFTF's MFF-3 and MFF-5 tests (U-10Zr in HT9), breaches were "strongly influenced by a large amount of fuel/cladding chemical interaction (FCCI)" concentrated at the top of the fuel column, which correlates with temperature and burnup. — [Carmack et al., J. Nucl. Mater. (2016), "Metallography and fuel cladding chemical interaction in FFTF irradiated metallic U-10Zr MFF-3 and MFF-5 fuel pins" (PNNL-hosted)](https://www.pnnl.gov/sites/default/files/media/file/Metallography%20and%20fuel%20cladding%20chemical%20interaction%20in%20fast%20flux%20test%20facility%20irradiated%20metallic%20U-10Zr%20MFF-3%20and%20MFF-5%20fuel%20pins.pdf) [IND-ACAD]

**Sodium-bonded fuel at the back end**
- EBR-II fuel "is not considered suitable for direct disposal ... because it contains sodium," which would react with water to make hydrogen. DOE chose pyroprocessing to treat 3.35 t of sodium-bonded EBR-II and FFTF fuel. In 2006 it estimated the remaining 2.65 t would take eight years and $234M, about $88,000/kg. — [IPFM 2010, ch. 7](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]
- NASEM 2023 Finding 15 says sodium-bonded fuel "is not suitable for direct disposal" and needs treatment methods not yet mature at industrial scale. It adds that SFRs "would produce large volumes of irradiated sodium waste." — [NASEM, "Merits and Viability of Different Nuclear Fuel Cycles...," 2023, Summary, DOI 10.17226/26500](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26500/chapter/3) [IND-NAS]
- EBR-II HALEU recovery:
  - DOE reports that nearly 5 t of HALEU has been recovered from EBR-II spent fuel. The target is about 10 t by Dec 2028, with half allocated to Oklo. — [DOE](https://www.energy.gov/node/4832691) [IND-GOV]; [ANS Nuclear Newswire, "Leading the charge: INL's role in advancing HALEU production," 24 Oct 2025](https://www.ans.org/news/2025-10-24/article-7387/leading-the-charge-inls-role-in-advancing-haleu-production/) [IND-PRESS]
  - The process is electrorefining in molten salt, then vacuum distillation, then down-blending to below 20% U-235. — same sources.

**Natrium fuel forms**
- Natrium will start on "Type 1" fuel, a U-10Zr alloy enriched below 20% with a sodium-bonded fuel column. The pins closely resemble EBR-II and FFTF pins. "Type 1B" is being qualified through a Lead Test Assembly program for longer residence time, higher burnup and higher outlet temperature. — [Technical Overview of Recent Developments in SMRs in the United States, arXiv:2504.02599 (2025)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.02599) [IND-ACAD, preprint]
- TerraPower's slides filed with NRC (2021–22) describe Type 1B as follows. — [TerraPower slides, NRC ADAMS ML22336A107](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2233/ML22336A107.pdf) [CO]
  - Annular uranium-metal fuel with a helium-filled central pore and no sodium bond.
  - An FCCI barrier and 75% smear density.
  - Demo-plant reloads of 16.5%-enriched uranium to 150 MWd/kg.
- A 2026 trade blog describes Natrium fuel as U-10Zr enriched to "~19.75%". — [Neutron Bytes (D. Yurman), "Nuclear Fuel Deals Frame the Future of Advanced Reactors," 8 Aug 2026](https://neutronbytes.com/2026/08/08/nuclear-fuel-deals-frame-the-future-of-advanced-reactors/) [IND-PRESS]
- EBR-II "operated initially with metallic HEU fuel." — [IPFM 2010, ch. 7 p. 93](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf) [IND-NGO]

### Inferences
- **Type 1 fuel.** The irradiation database for sodium-bonded U-10Zr in HT9-type cladding is substantial, at up to about 20 at% in EBR-II and FFTF. Natrium Type 1 is deliberately close to that design, which is the strongest evidence for its fuel. Two caveats apply:
  - The legacy database was built largely with HEU and Pu-bearing driver fuels. HALEU-only cores operate at a different fissile density and power history.
  - NRC has accepted qualification plans only as "feedback," and ACRS explicitly left eutectic-melt (FCCI) margins for the operating-license stage.
- **Type 1B fuel.** Type 1B (unbonded, annular, with an FCCI barrier) has far less irradiation history. Its qualification is a future step, not a demonstrated result.
- **Back-end liability.** Choosing sodium-bonded fuel carries a known back-end problem. The only US precedent, about 3 t of EBR-II/FFTF fuel, has been in treatment since the 1990s, with a 2028 completion target for the driver fuel. Per-kg cost was high: DOE's 2006 estimate was about $88,000/kg.

### Gaps
- No public results were found for 2024–2026 Natrium or Aurora fuel irradiation tests in INL's ATR or TREAT. An INL scope document reportedly covers examination of "LDC-1B" specimens, but it was not opened.
- No NRC approval letter for any Natrium fuel topical report was found.
- Natrium Type 1 burnup and peak-temperature limits, and its eutectic-margin numbers, were not found.
- Aurora's fuel design (enrichment, cladding, smear density, burnup target) was not found in any independent source.

## 3. Natrium: NRC safety evaluation, ACRS findings, open items, exemptions, molten-salt storage, seismic isolation, design changes

### Takeaway
NRC issued the Kemmerer Unit 1 construction permit on 9 Mar 2026, after a final safety evaluation in Dec 2025 and an ACRS letter of 16 Nov 2025 that supported issuance. The permit means NRC judged the plant can be built as designed. It is not a finding that the plant is safe to operate. ACRS named eight areas needing special attention at the operating-license stage, including functional containment, reactivity accidents, passive-cooling validation, sodium fires, seismic design, and uncertainties and margins. It called the sodium-salt heat exchanger "one of the least mature elements of the plant." Completion is now targeted for 2030, three years later than DOE's original ARDP target.

**ACRS letter, 16 Nov 2025 (ML25311A150)** — all items below from [ACRS letter](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2531/ML25311A150.pdf); [ACRS 730th meeting summary, 11 Dec 2025, ML25342A445](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2534/ML25342A445.pdf) [IND-REG]
- **Safety features it credits:**
  - two means of passive heat removal;
  - two diverse means of generating scrams;
  - "significant separation between the sodium and steam systems";
  - "The design does not credit electrical power or operator intervention to achieve a safe shutdown."
- **Methodology:** Implementing LMP at this stage is "acceptable."
- **Construction finding:** ACRS agrees with staff that the plant "can be constructed in accordance with relevant regulations and the design bases outlined in the preliminary safety analysis report."
- **Areas warranting special attention at the OL stage:**
  - "implementation of the functional containment approach";
  - "the system response to reactivity accidents";
  - "validation of the passive cooling design";
  - "completion of design features to prevent or mitigate sodium fires";
  - "seismic design";
  - "integration of the completed PRA and defense-in-depth assessments";
  - "evaluation of uncertainties";
  - "quantification of safety margins".
- **Severe accidents.** TerraPower will argue at the OL stage that hypothetical core disruptive accidents (HCDAs) are incredible, so their consequences "will not be specifically considered." ACRS: "We plan to further evaluate the justification when we review the OL application." ACRS's footnote notes that an IAEA study (TECDOC-2006) assumed 350 kg of sodium forced into containment by an HCDA.
- **Bounding transients.** Safety analyses assume only one control rod withdraws and that the pump rotor coasts down. More severe cases are handled by preventive features. ACRS calls evaluating "a dual locked-rotor scenario" an "important detail for the OL application." Those cases are:
  - multiple rod withdrawal, prevented by a non-safety rod interlock;
  - locked-rotor pump failure, mitigated by bypass flow.
- **Passive cooling.** Both decay-heat systems (the reactor air cooling (RAC) and intermediate air cooling (IAC) systems) rely on natural circulation. Their models "will need to be validated," and because they depend on long air passages that external events could block, "a means to reliably keep such air passages open will need to be demonstrated."
- **Functional containment.** Compared with prior SFRs, it uses "higher leak rate limits ... and non-safety designations for certain components of the enveloping barriers." ACRS judges it "reasonable to expect" this can be justified at the OL stage.
- **Sodium fires.** "Given the difficulty in fighting sodium fires, more details about mitigating barriers, sodium fire progression models, and the fire protection program are needed."
- **Sodium-salt heat exchanger.** Radionuclide retention depends on the sodium-salt heat exchanger being a reliable pressure boundary. "The heat exchanger design concept is innovative but remains one of the least mature elements of the plant." Separating the energy island "essentially eliminates the potential for sodium-water interaction," but there is "potential for sodium-salt interaction."
- **Seismic isolation.** It is "novel." It follows the 1980s GE PRISM concept of steel and rubber isolators. Two issues need attention:
  - "relative deflections" between isolated and non-isolated structures;
  - "reactivity response to seismic forces ... may be relatively new for an SFR design."
- **Reactivity coefficients.** They depend on complex fuel-deformation and core-restraint calculations, so "3-D kinetics may be necessary." The uncertainties and "the margin to avoiding a net positive reactivity coefficient should be quantified."
- **Low-frequency scenario screen.** TerraPower will evaluate scenarios with frequency ≥1×10⁻⁷/yr at 95% confidence, or with dose >1,000 rem total effective dose over 30 days at the exclusion area boundary. It reports no scenario yet reaching that dose, "even considering ... unprotected reactivity addition and unprotected loss of flow."

### Inferences
- **What the permit establishes.** The permit and the ACRS letter establish regulatory confidence that the design basis is coherent enough to build. They explicitly leave the decisive operating-safety demonstrations to the operating-license stage:
  - that HCDAs are incredible;
  - functional containment with higher leak rates;
  - quantified margins to a positive reactivity coefficient and to eutectic melt;
  - validated natural-circulation air cooling;
  - sodium-fire protection;
  - seismic-isolation behaviour.
- **Response to historical failure modes.** Natrium answers the main historical SFR failure mode, sodium-water reactions in steam generators (BN-350, Phénix; §1), by putting salt between sodium and steam. That moves the novel interface to a sodium-salt heat exchanger, which ACRS calls among the least mature parts of the plant. The heat exchanger matters for safety because radionuclide retention depends on it.
- **Thermal storage.** The concentrating solar power record shows that hot-salt tank and piping leaks can cause long outages and destroy project economics (Crescent Dunes), while well-executed plants (Gemasolar) operate for years. For Natrium, salt-system failures would mainly be availability and revenue risks, because the energy island is non-nuclear. Sodium-salt heat exchanger leaks, however, are a safety-case item per ACRS.
- **Review duration.** Docketing to final safety evaluation took about 19 months; docketing to permit took about 21.5 months (computed from NRC dates).

## 4. Aurora: the 2022 NRC denial, what has changed, EBR-II heritage vs scale, recycling/pyroprocessing, surplus plutonium

### Takeaway
NRC denied Oklo's 2020 combined license application in Jan 2022 on procedural grounds. Oklo had repeatedly failed to provide substantive information on its maximum credible accident, its safety classification of structures, systems and components, and other issues. NRC made no safety finding. Since then the design has grown from about 1.5 MWe to 75 MWe. The NRC approved only Oklo's Principal Design Criteria topical report (final safety evaluation 22 Apr 2026). The first unit, Aurora-INL, is being authorized by DOE under the Reactor Pilot Program, not licensed by NRC; DOE approved its preliminary safety analysis in Jun 2026, with the final step still pending. Aurora's claimed EBR-II heritage spans a scale-up from 20 MWe to 75 MWe. Its recycling and plutonium plans are at negotiation or pre-application stage and face independent proliferation, cost and waste critiques.

### Inferences
- **Independent review.** The substance behind the 2022 denial has not yet been tested by NRC for the 75 MWe design: how the maximum credible accident is defined, and how components are safety-classified. The only NRC safety evaluation for Aurora is of design criteria. The first unit's safety basis is being reviewed by DOE, which is also Aurora-INL's host, fuel supplier and program sponsor. That is a different level of independence from NRC licensing.
- **Scale.** EBR-II converted 62.5 MWt to 20 MWe, about 32% efficiency. At 32–40% efficiency, a 75 MWe Aurora implies about 190–235 MWt, roughly 3–4 times EBR-II's thermal power. This is an estimate, because no source states Aurora's MWt.
- **Fuel differences.** EBR-II's own passive-safety tests (1986) used HEU metal fuel. Aurora's HALEU core, and any future plutonium or transuranic core, differs in neutronics. ACRS's Natrium letter notes that Pu-free fuel reduces void reactivity worth, so Pu-bearing Aurora fuel would face a larger void-worth question and would need new fuel qualification.
- **Recycling evidence.** The recycling claims (90% energy use, smaller volume, lower radiotoxicity, proliferation resistance) are company statements. Independent sources weigh against several of them, or at least say they are unproven at commercial scale: NASEM (repository still required; separation raises risk), the Bulletin-cited lab study (modest improvement over PUREX) and IPFM (DOE's ~$88,000/kg treatment cost; slow throughput).

## 5. HTGR/TRISO: AVR, THTR-300, Fort St. Vrain, Peach Bottom 1, HTR-10 and HTR-PM; AGR results; the EPRI topical report; pebble-bed issues; NRC review of Xe-100

### Takeaway
UCO TRISO fuel has the strongest independent qualification basis of the three designs. DOE's AGR-1 and AGR-2 programs showed very low particle failure, with no failures at 1,600–1,700 °C for 300 h in AGR-1. NRC approved EPRI's topical report on this fuel in 2020, but only within a defined envelope of particle properties. The reactor-system record is weaker:
- AVR's core ran hotter than predicted and was contaminated.
- THTR-300 broke pebbles and closed in 1989 after 423 full-load days.
- Fort St. Vrain had poor availability.
- China's HTR-PM, the closest analog, entered commercial operation in Dec 2023 and passed loss-of-cooling tests at 200 MWt. Its PRIS load factor was 15.6% in 2024 and 43.8% in 2025, and its fuel-handling system had commissioning problems.

NRC issued the Long Mott construction permit (four Xe-100 units) on 8–9 Nov 2026, and licensed the TRISO-X fuel plant on 13 Feb 2026.

  - AGR-1 and AGR-2 testing "constitutes a performance demonstration of these particle designs over a range of normal operating and off-normal accident conditions."
  - The approval is valid for particle-layer properties within the report's envelope (Table 5-5).
  - "Further fuel qualification efforts for other aspects of the fuel or a broader scope of performance conditions could be within the scope of future submittals."
- X-energy characterizes AGR results as "zero coating failures across 300,000 particles after three years at 1,250 °C," 19% FIMA burnup and fission-product retention to 1,800 °C. It says "the fuel itself becomes the primary containment system" and that the Xe-100's maximum accident temperature is "well below" 1,600 °C. — [X-energy, "TRISO-X: Advanced Fuel for Next-Generation Nuclear Energy," 10 Jun 2026](https://x-energy.com/?p=11536) [CO]

### Inferences
- **Fuel versus reactor.** Of the three designs, the Xe-100's fuel has the most independent, NRC-accepted evidence (AGR-1/2 via EPRI-AR-1(NP)-A, plus AGR-5/6/7). That evidence covers lab-fabricated particles within a property envelope. Factory-scale TRISO-X production must reproduce it, and NRC said other fuel aspects may need further submittals. Fuel strength does not address the reactor-system issues behind past pebble-bed problems:
  - pebble handling and breakage (THTR, HTR-PM commissioning);
  - core-temperature uncertainty (AVR);
  - graphite dust and graphite waste;
  - water and air ingress.
- **HTR-PM availability.** HTR-PM shows a commercial pebble-bed plant can be built, connected and passively cooled from 200 MWt. Its early operation shows low availability:
  - In 2024 it averaged about 33 MWe while online (206,000 MWh over 6,329 h), roughly 22% of its 150 MWe reference.
  - In 2025 that rose to about 127 MWe (576,000 MWh over 4,552 h).
  - Its reference rating (150 MWe) is below design (200 MWe).
- **Applicability to Xe-100.** These HTR-PM results are evidence about the technology class. The Xe-100 itself (one 200 MWt module per reactor, X-energy's own pebble handling) has no operating unit.
- **Temperature uncertainty.** AVR's lesson, that pebble-bed core temperatures could not be measured and were underpredicted, bears directly on X-energy's claim that accident temperatures stay "well below" 1,600 °C. That margin rests on validated core thermal models, not on in-core measurement.

- HTR-10 operating data beyond a 10 MWt rating and 700 °C outlet were not verified.
- HTR-PM's actual operating outlet temperature, the reasons for its low 2024 output and its 150 vs 200 MWe derating were not found.
- Xe-100 helium outlet temperature, pebble inventory (sources conflict, about 200,000 vs 220,000), burnup and per-module HALEU needs were not verified independently.
- Long Mott items not found:

  - the substance of the environmental review exemptions.
- AGR-2 final failure fractions and the final AGR-5/6/7 qualification report were not retrieved.
- No independent quantification of graphite dust or air and water ingress was found for the Xe-100.

## 6. HALEU: actual production, DOE allocations, Russian ban/waivers, enrichment and deconversion plans, and demand vs supply

### Takeaway
The only operating US HALEU cascade, Centrus's 16-machine unit at Piketon, has produced about 0.9 t/yr. Cumulative output was more than 920 kg by Jun 2025 and more than 1,900 kg by mid-Jun 2026, per Centrus; IPFM questions the second tranche. For comparison, a secondary source puts one Natrium first core at 15–20 t of HALEU metal. New commercial HALEU enrichment (Centrus's planned 12 t/yr expansion, General Matter) is not expected before about 2029. Commercial deconversion "does not exist," per Centrus's CEO. Near-term cores therefore depend on DOE material and on EBR-II-recovered HALEU (Oklo's 5 t). Waivers for Russian imports end on 1 Jan 2028, and Russia itself has restricted exports to the US since Nov 2024.

### Inferences
- **Scale of the shortfall.** At the demonstrated ~0.9 t/yr, current US commercial enrichment output would take roughly 17–22 years to enrich a single 15–20 t Natrium first core. That figure ignores deconversion and metallization, which do not yet exist at commercial scale.
- **Natrium.** A 2030 completion therefore requires DOE-supplied HALEU in quantities DOE has not disclosed (likely down-blended HEU), new enrichment that starts around 2029 at the earliest, or foreign supply (ASP Isotopes, not yet built). This is the single largest non-technical dependency common to Natrium and Xe-100.
- **Xe-100.** TX-1 is sized for about 11 reactors, roughly 64,000 pebbles per reactor per year (700,000 ÷ 11). It will need HALEU from 2028, but X-energy's enrichment partners' new capacity is not yet operating.
- **Aurora.** Aurora-INL's first core is insulated from this shortage by its EBR-II allocation. That allocation is finite, about 10 t total recoverable by 2028 with half to Oklo. Additional Aurora units would need commercial HALEU, recycled fuel or plutonium, none of which is available yet.

## 7. Independent critiques on safety, waste and proliferation (NASEM 2023, UCS 2021, Krall/Macfarlane/Ewing 2022, IAEA-related)

### Takeaway
The three main independent critiques agree in direction:
- NASEM (2023): non-LWR advanced reactors face major hurdles to commercial deployment by 2050; no fuel cycle removes the need for a geologic repository; HALEU expansion raises proliferation and security risk; SFRs and HTGRs create distinctive wastes (irradiated sodium, sodium-bonded fuel, large spent-fuel volumes, irradiated graphite).
- UCS (Lyman, 2021): non-LWR designs are "not likely to be significantly safer," and SFRs are worse on safety and security.
- Krall et al. (PNAS 2022): small cores increase waste volumes; the paper was disputed by industry and drew an editor's note on a claimed factual error.

### Cited Findings
**NASEM 2023 (DOI 10.17226/26500)** — [NASEM, "Merits and Viability of Different Nuclear Fuel Cycles and Technology Options and the Waste Aspects of Advanced Nuclear Reactors," 2023, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26500/chapter/3); [catalog page](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/26500/merits-and-viability-of-different-nuclear-fuel-cycles-and-technology-options-and-the-waste-aspects-of-advanced-nuclear-reactors) [IND-NAS]
- Finding 4: non-LWR designs "will confront significant challenges in meeting commercial deployment by 2050," with "no currently operating fueled prototypes."
- Finding 10: costs run from "at least several billion dollars" for pilot non-LWRs and fuel-cycle facilities to "hundreds of billions of dollars" for alternative fuel cycles.
- Finding 13: higher-burnup fuels increase repository thermal loads.
- Finding 19: HALEU expansion "would potentially exacerbate proliferation and security risks."
- Recommendations:
  - D: continue direct disposal "for the foreseeable future";
  - F: independent assessment of cost estimates;
  - M and O: HALEU risk assessment, and NRC rulemaking on HALEU security and material accounting.
- The American Nuclear Society's response agreed that the HALEU shortage is "an important impediment." — [ANS letter to National Academies, 10 Mar 2023](https://ans.org/file/10469/03.10.23+-+ANS+Letter+to+National+Academies+on+AR+Fuel+Cycle+Report.pdf) [professional society]

**UCS 2021** — [UCS (E. Lyman), "'Advanced' Isn't Always Better," 18 Mar 2021](https://www.ucs.org/resources/advanced-isnt-always-better); [UCS press release](https://www.ucs.org/about/news/report-advanced-nuclear-reactors-no-better-current-fleet) [IND-NGO]
- Non-LWR designs are "not likely to be significantly safer than today's nuclear plants," and some "pose even more safety, proliferation, and environmental risks."
- SFRs and molten-salt-fueled reactors are "significantly worse" than LWRs on safety and security.
- SFRs would likely be less uranium-efficient and would not reduce the waste needing long-term isolation.
- Natrium's HALEU start carries lower proliferation risk than plutonium or HEU.
- UCS recommended that DOE pause ARDP until NRC decides whether full-scale prototype testing is needed before commercial licensing.

**Krall, Macfarlane and Ewing, PNAS, 31 May 2022 (DOI 10.1073/pnas.2111833119)**
- Because small cores leak more neutrons, "SMRs will exacerbate the challenges of nuclear waste management." Most SMR designs would increase waste volume "by factors of 2 to 30" relative to the reference reactors. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9191363) [IND-ACAD]
- NuScale and GE Hitachi disputed the paper. — [POWER](https://www.powermag.com/researchers-say-smrs-will-produce-more-waste-than-large-nuclear-reactors-nuscale-disputes-claim/); [S&P Global, Jun 2022](https://spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/articles/2022/6/small-modular-reactor-firms-cast-doubt-on-nuclear-waste-study-70638423) [IND-PRESS, quoting CO]
- The PNAS editor-in-chief reportedly said there was "a factual error in the paper" about NuScale spent-fuel quantities. No formal correction was found. — [Bloomberg Government](https://news.bgov.com/states-of-play/mini-nuclear-reactors-land-in-scientific-spat-over-waste-output) [IND-PRESS]

**IAEA-related and other critiques**
- ACRS cites IAEA TECDOC-2006 (2022), which assumed 350 kg of sodium forced into containment by an SFR HCDA. — [ACRS letter, 16 Nov 2025](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2531/ML25311A150.pdf) [IND-REG]
- GRS cites an IAEA warning against calling whole plants "inherently safe." — [GRS, 11 Oct 2024](https://www.grs.de/en/news/pebble-bed-reactor-shidaowan-nuclear-power-plant-inherently-safe-reactor)
- Moniz, Markey, Beyer and Garamendi have criticized the plutonium transfers (see §4). — [Arms Control Today, Jul/Aug 2026](https://www.armscontrol.org/node/15388) [IND-NGO]

### Inferences
- **Consistency with regulators.** The independent critiques line up with the regulators' open items rather than contradicting them. ACRS's Natrium list (HCDA, containment, reactivity margins, sodium fires) and NRC's bounded TRISO approval are the same issues UCS and NASEM emphasize.
- **What NASEM and UCS do not address.** They predate the 2026 construction permits and the HTR-PM operating record, so they do not assess those newer facts.
- **Krall et al.** The paper's quantitative claims are contested, with an editor's note but no correction found. Its physical point about neutron leakage in small cores is not in dispute. Its relevance to these three designs is indirect.

### Gaps
- The full UCS chapters on SFRs and HTGRs (sodium void numbers, pebble-bed critiques) were not retrieved; only summary pages were.
- The exact text of the PNAS editor's note, and any author reply, were not found.
- Krall et al.'s case-study designs were not confirmed in this session; reportedly they include an SFR (Toshiba 4S).
- No IAEA review specific to Natrium, Aurora or Xe-100 was found.

## 8. Cost evidence: first-of-a-kind estimates, overruns and independent cost studies

### Takeaway
Independent cost evidence is sparse and points to first-of-a-kind (FOAK) costs well above targets:
- NuScale/UAMPS was cancelled in Nov 2023 after its price target rose to $89/MWh.
- Natrium is estimated at about $4bn (DOE share about $2bn).
- DOE's federal commitment to Xe-100/Long Mott rose from $1.2bn (2021) to about $2.15bn.
- DOE's 2023 Liftoff report and INL's 2024 meta-analysis put FOAK and early overnight costs at about $5,500–10,000/kWe for SMRs, against roughly $3,600/kW needed for scale.
- Historical SFR and HTGR costs were well above LWRs: CEA's 2.58× ratio for Superphénix, and about 20% higher power cost for HTR-PM.

### Inferences
- **Natrium.** $4bn for 345 MWe is about $11,600/kWe on a total-project basis. That is above Liftoff's $6,200/kW FOAK benchmark and the INL SMR range. The comparison is not like-for-like: the $4bn includes the storage island, first-of-a-kind engineering and possibly fuel facilities, and the basis of the estimate was not found.
- **Xe-100.** The federal commitment rose from $1.2bn to about $2.15bn, about 80%. At up to 50% cost share, total project cost has likely risen proportionately. The ARDP scope reportedly includes the TRISO-X plant, so a $/kWe figure for the reactors alone cannot be derived.
- **Precedent.** The NuScale cancellation is the clearest US precedent: FOAK SMR cost growth at the pre-construction stage was enough to end a project with DOE support.

### Gaps
- No GAO or DOE Inspector General report after 2022 on ARDP cost growth was found.
- No independent (non-company) bottom-up estimate was found for Natrium, Xe-100 or Aurora.
- Oklo has published no Aurora capital-cost estimate in the sources found.
- The $9.3bn NuScale/UAMPS total-cost figure could not be verified.
- DOE Liftoff 2024 updated $/kW figures were not confirmed.

## 9. Cross-examination: contradictions among TerraPower, Oklo and X-energy claims, and against independent evidence

### Takeaway
The largest conflicts are:
- **Schedules.** Company dates have repeatedly slipped (Natrium 2027→2030; Aurora's operation date has moved several times). Only Long Mott's NRC review finished faster than forecast.
- **Fuel availability.** Companies' bets on HALEU by the early 2030s sit against NASEM's "no domestic capacity" finding and Centrus's actual ~0.9 t/yr.
- **Passive-safety and "fuel as containment" claims.** These run ahead of what NRC and ACRS have actually accepted, and of the AVR and HTR-PM record.
- **Waste and recycling.** Oklo's claims conflict with NASEM and other independent assessments.
- **Plutonium.** TerraPower's own safety case credits Pu-free fuel for reducing void reactivity worth, while Oklo pursues plutonium fuel.

## 10. Per-design evidence assessment: what is demonstrated, who verified it, what remains unproven, which limits apply, and does each design stand on solid ground?

### Takeaway
None of the three designs has an operating prototype of its own. NASEM's 2023 Finding 4 ("no currently operating fueled prototypes") still holds in Nov 2026 for Natrium and the Xe-100, and for Aurora pending DOE start-up.
- **Natrium** rests on well-established SFR physics and metal-fuel data, and has the deepest independent review (NRC final safety evaluation, ACRS letter, construction permit). Its novel interfaces and HALEU supply are unproven.
- **Xe-100** rests on the best-qualified fuel (AGR program, NRC-approved EPRI report) and a foreign operating analog (HTR-PM) whose early availability is poor. It has an NRC construction permit as of Nov 2026.
- **Aurora** rests mainly on the EBR-II heritage argument. It has the least independent safety review: NRC has approved design criteria only, and its first unit is under DOE authorization.

### Cited Findings
**Natrium**
- Demonstrated (by others, earlier):
  - EBR-II passive shutdown, 1986 (ANL). — [OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/5428837)
  - Metallic fuel at about 15–20 at% burnup in EBR-II and FFTF. — [PNNL-hosted review](https://www.pnnl.gov/sites/default/files/media/file/Irradiation%20Behavior%20of%20Metallic%20Fast%20Reactor%20Fuels.pdf)
  - Large-pool SFR operation at a 66–74% load factor (BN-600/800). — [WNA/PRIS](https://world-nuclear.org/nuclear-reactor-database/details/BELOYARSK-4); [IPPE 2018](https://gif.jaea.go.jp/webinar/Series24/gifiv_webinar_pakhomov_19_dec_2018_final.pdf)
- Verified by NRC and ACRS: constructible design basis, and an acceptable LMP implementation at the construction-permit stage. — [ACRS, 16 Nov 2025](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2531/ML25311A150.pdf); [NRC](https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/new-reactors/advanced/who-were-working-with/applicant-projects/terrapower.html)
- Unproven, per ACRS: — [ACRS](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2531/ML25311A150.pdf)
  - functional containment;
  - exclusion of HCDAs;
  - reactivity margins;
  - passive-cooling validation;
  - sodium-fire design;
  - seismic isolation;
  - the sodium-salt heat exchanger;
  - fuel thermomechanical margins.
- Physical limits: — [IPFM 2010](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf)
  - sodium's chemical reactivity with air and water;
  - opaque coolant, which slows inspection and repair;
  - void reactivity worth in fast spectra;
  - eutectic melt and FCCI limits in metal fuel.

**Xe-100**
- Demonstrated:
  - UCO TRISO performance in AGR-1 and AGR-2. — [NRC SE](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2021/ML20210M241.pdf)
  - Low AGR-5/6/7 failure fractions. — [INL](https://inldigitallibrary.inl.gov/sites/sti/sti/Sort_47193.pdf)
  - HTR-PM passive cooldown from 200 MWt. — [Joule 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2024.06.014)
- Verified: NRC construction permit (Nov 2026) and TRISO-X Part 70 license (Feb 2026). — [ANS](https://www.ans.org/news/article-8573/nrc-issues-construction-permit-for-dowxenergy-long-mott-project/); [DOE](https://www.energy.gov/node/4856584)
- Unproven:
  - Xe-100 reactor operation and pebble handling;
  - factory-scale TRISO-X quality;
  - HTR-PM-like availability, at 15.6% and 43.8% load factor in 2024–25. — [WNA/PRIS](https://world-nuclear.org/nuclear-reactor-database/details/Shidaowan-HTR-PM-1)
- Limits:
  - pebble-bed temperature uncertainty (AVR); — [Moormann](https://hdl.handle.net/2128/3136)
  - graphite dust and waste, and large spent-fuel volumes; — [NASEM](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26500/chapter/3)
  - water and air ingress. — [GRS](https://www.grs.de/en/news/pebble-bed-reactor-shidaowan-nuclear-power-plant-inherently-safe-reactor)

**Aurora**
- Demonstrated: EBR-II's 30-year operation at 62.5 MWt. — [IPFM](https://fissilematerials.org/library/rr08.pdf)
- Verified:
  - NRC approval of the Principal Design Criteria topical report; — [Oklo PR](https://oklo.com/newsroom/news-details/2026/Oklos-NRC-Principal-Design-Criteria-Topical-Report-Approved-for-Aurora-Powerhouse-in-Idaho/default.aspx) [CO]
  - DOE PDSA approval. — [ANS](https://www.ans.org/news/2026-06-12/article-8119/oklo-secures-doe-pdsa-approval/)
- Unproven:
  - NRC review of its accident analysis; the 2022 denial was over the maximum credible accident and safety classification; — [FR](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2022-01-11/html/2022-00339.htm)
  - scale-up to 75 MWe;
  - recycling and plutonium fuel. — [NASEM](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26500/chapter/3); [ACA](https://www.armscontrol.org/node/15388)
- Limits: same SFR limits as Natrium.

### Inferences
- **Natrium** stands on solid ground for reactor physics and the basic fuel type. The reasons:
  - Metal-fuel SFR passive response was demonstrated at EBR-II.
  - The fuel database is close to the Type 1 design.
  - NRC and ACRS found the safety approach acceptable at the construction-permit stage.
  
  It does not yet stand on solid ground for the integrated first-of-a-kind plant. The reasons:
  - ACRS lists eight unresolved operating-license areas and calls the sodium-salt heat exchanger among the least mature elements.
  - The SFR fleet's reliability record outside Russia is poor.
  - The HALEU first core, about 15–20 t per a secondary source, exceeds total US commercial HALEU production to date (about 1.9 t) by about an order of magnitude.
- **Xe-100** stands on solid ground for fuel. The reasons:
  - There is NRC-accepted AGR evidence, now reinforced by AGR-5/6/7.
  - HTR-PM's 2023 tests empirically show the passive decay-heat removal concept works at 200 MWt.
  
  Its weaker ground is operational:
  - HTR-PM availability has been low.
  - Pebble handling problems and AVR temperature uncertainty recur across pebble-bed history.
  - Graphite and spent-fuel volumes are back-end burdens.
  - TX-1 output depends on HALEU enrichment that is not yet built.
- **Aurora** has the thinnest independent evidence base, for four reasons:
  - Its heritage claim spans a roughly 3–4× thermal scale-up (an estimate; Aurora's MWt is unpublished) and a change from HEU to HALEU fuel.
  - NRC has not reviewed its accident analysis since denying the 2022 application.
  - First-unit authorization is by DOE.
  - Its long-term fuel strategy (recycling, plutonium) depends on technologies that independent bodies judge immature, costly or proliferation-sensitive.
  
  Its near-term strength is that its first core is secured from EBR-II HALEU, so it is less exposed to the commercial HALEU gap than Natrium or the Xe-100.

### Gaps
- No independent probabilistic risk assessment results (core-damage or large-release frequencies) were found for any of the three designs.
- No independent availability forecasts were found.
- For Aurora, no thermal power, transient analyses or fuel-qualification data were found in independent sources, so its physical margins cannot be assessed from public evidence.
